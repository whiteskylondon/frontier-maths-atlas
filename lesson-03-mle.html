Lesson 03 · Estimation & inference

Maximum likelihood, done properly

Pick the parameter that makes the data you saw most probable — then ask what that estimate is worth.

Level US Senior undergrad → early graduate FR Master M1 · ENSAE 2A

Maximum likelihood answers one question: given the data, which value of the parameter would have made it most probable? You write the probability of the sample as a function of θ, then climb to its peak. The payoff here is a loose end tied: the MLE of a normal's variance is exactly the biased ÷n estimator from Lesson 2 — so now you'll see where that estimator came from, and why "most likely" and "unbiased" are different goals.

The likelihood and the score

You maximise the log-likelihood — same peak, but sums beat products. At an interior maximum its derivative, the score, is zero:

  • the likelihood — the sample's probability read as a function of θ, data fixed. Not a probability over θ; it needn't integrate to 1.
  • the log-likelihood. The product becomes a sum, which is why the log is used; the argmax is unchanged.
  • the score — slope of ℓ. The MLE is the root of the estimating equation U(θ̂)=0: the flat point at the top.
  • Fisher information = Var(U) = −E[ℓ″(θ)]. The curvature of ℓ at the peak: a sharper peak means the data pin θ down more tightly.

The precise statement

Under regularity conditions, the MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal: √n(θ̂ₙ − θ₀) →ᵈ 𝒩(0, I₁(θ₀)⁻¹), reaching the Cramér–Rao lower bound in the limit — asymptotically, no estimator does better. It is not guaranteed unbiased in finite samples, as σ̂² shows.

What the estimate is worth — properties
  • Consistent. θ̂ₙ → θ₀ as n → ∞. The peak walks onto the truth.
  • Asymptotically normal & efficient. Its spread is I₁(θ₀)⁻¹/n — the smallest any (regular) estimator can achieve in the limit. More Fisher information ⇒ tighter estimate ⇒ smaller standard error.
  • Invariant. The MLE of g(θ) is g(θ̂). Estimate σ², read off σ̂ = √σ̂² for free.
  • But not finite-sample unbiased. σ̂² = S²ₙ is biased low; MLE optimises likelihood, not bias. Asymptotically the bias washes out.

The twist — when the guarantees quietly fail

Consistency and asymptotic normality rest on regularity conditions, and they can break. For Uniform(0, θ) the MLE is θ̂ = max(xᵢ): it is biased (E = nθ/(n+1)), converges at rate n rather than √n, and is not asymptotically normal — because the support depends on θ, so you can't differentiate under the integral. With many nuisance parameters (Neyman–Scott) the MLE can even be inconsistent. "Maximum likelihood" is a default worth reaching for, not a theorem that holds unconditionally — always check the conditions before trusting the standard errors.

Why this matters · volarixs

MLE is the engine under most of your fitting. Fat-tailed returns → a Student-t fit by maximum likelihood; volatility → GARCH by (quasi-)MLE; every classifier trained on cross-entropy is doing MLE, since cross-entropy = −log-likelihood. Recognising that unifies "loss functions" and "estimation" into one object.

The operational payoff is Fisher information → standard errors on fitted coefficients. Whether a signal's fitted loading is distinguishable from zero is a statement about the curvature of its likelihood — the same machinery as the confidence intervals in Lesson 1, and the honest input to the multiple-testing correction in your pre-registration. And the twist is a live warning: fit too many parameters on too little history and the finite-sample bias and inflated errors bite exactly where you're trying to read a faint edge.
