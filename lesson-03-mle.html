Lesson 03 · Wing 0 — statistical & linear-algebra foundations

Maximum likelihood, done properly

Pick the parameter that makes the data you saw most probable — then ask what that estimate is worth.

Level US Senior undergrad → graduate FR Master 1 · ENSAE 2A

You watched an index for a year: 250 daily returns. You believe a model — each day's move is a draw from a bell curve with some centre μ and some spread σ — but nobody tells you the two numbers. Maximum likelihood is the rule for choosing them: for each candidate pair, ask "how probable was the year I actually saw, if this pair were the truth?", and keep the pair that makes your data most probable. That probability-read-backwards is called the likelihood. The payoff ties Lesson 2's loose end: for the bell curve the recipe has closed forms, and the spread it picks is exactly the biased ÷n estimator — "most likely" and "unbiased" are different gods.

The vocabulary, in plain words
  • likelihoodthe probability (density) of the sample you actually saw, viewed as a function of the candidate parameters — data frozen, parameters varying. Not a distribution over the parameters: it needn't integrate to 1 in θ, which is why the word is likelihood, not probability.
  • log-likelihood ℓthe log of that. Same peak — log is increasing — but 250 multiplied densities become 250 added terms. Kinder to calculus (derivatives of sums) and to floating point (no underflow).
  • estimator vs estimatethe recipe vs the number it returns on your data. μ̂ and σ̂ wear hats because they are outputs of a recipe fed a random sample — hence random themselves, as in Lessons 1–2.
  • scorethe slope of ℓ. At an interior peak the slope is zero, so "set the score to zero" is the master equation of estimation. Every gradient-based fit in ML is climbing a (log-)likelihood or a close cousin.
  • Fisher informationthe curvature of ℓ at its peak. Sharp peak → the data pin the parameter down → small standard error. Flat peak → many parameter values explain the data about equally well.
  • profile likelihoodthe likelihood of one parameter after letting the others do their best at each of its values. The mountain seen from one axis: its ridge-line. This is how honest intervals for a single parameter are cut.
The data — one seeded year

Everything below runs on a single simulated year: n = 250 daily index returns in basis points, drawn once from a Gaussian with true drift μ = 3 bp/day and true vol σ = 100 bp/day (≈ 16% annualised — an ordinary equity-index year) using a fixed-seed generator, so the page shows the same year on every load. The page knows the truth; the estimator is never told.

From probability to likelihood

Independence turns the year's probability into a product of daily densities; the log turns the product into a sum. At an interior maximum the derivative — the score — vanishes:

  • the model's density at observed return xᵢ under parameters θ. Here θ = (μ, σ) and f is the Gaussian density.
  • the likelihood — the sample's joint density as a function of θ, data fixed. The i.i.d. assumption is what makes it a product.
  • the score — the gradient of ℓ. U(θ̂) = 0 is the estimating equation: the flat point at the summit.
  • the maximum-likelihood estimator. A statistic — a function of the sample — so it has a sampling distribution, standard error, bias.
The Gaussian pipeline — score equations to closed forms

Two parameters, two score equations, both solvable by hand — the rare model where the summit has coordinates in closed form:

  • the sample mean of the year's returns. The μ-score is linear in μ, so its root is unique and σ-free — remember this for the profile.
  • the MLE of the variance — divisor 1/n. Exactly Lesson 2's biased estimator: E[σ̂²] = ((n−1)/n)·σ².
  • the penalty for claiming a big σ. A wide bell "explains" everything weakly; this term is what stops σ̂ from running to infinity.

There is the loose end, tied: the likelihood's own peak divides by n, not n−1. Maximum likelihood optimises fit to the year you saw, not average correctness over the years you didn't. At n = 250 the expected shortfall is the factor (n−1)/n = 249/250 — σ² understated by 0.4% on average. The size is trivial; the principle is not.

Curvature is information — the Fisher matrix

How much should you trust the summit's coordinates? Look at how fast ℓ falls as you step away — the curvature. Averaged over data, that curvature is the Fisher information; inverted, it is the asymptotic variance of the estimator:

  • Fisher information of the whole sample: I = E[−∇²ℓ] = Var(U). Expected curvature of ℓ at the truth; n observations stack information linearly.
  • the off-diagonal — μ and σ are information-orthogonal in the Gaussian. Estimation errors of μ̂ and σ̂ are asymptotically uncorrelated; each can be treated alone.
  • asymptotic standard error, read off I⁻¹ with σ̂ plugged in. Var(θ̂) ≈ I_n(θ₀)⁻¹ — the sharper the peak, the smaller the error bar.

Plug the year in: SE(μ̂) = σ̂/√n ≈ … bp, so μ̂ sits only … standard errors from zero — one year of daily data cannot tell a 3 bp drift from no drift at all. But SE(σ̂) = σ̂/√(2n) ≈ … bp: the same year pins the vol to within a few percent. That √2 is why markets let you measure risk far better than reward.

One more gift, invariance: the MLE of any transform is the transform of the MLE. σ̂ = √σ̂² needs no new optimisation, and the desk's annualised vol √252·σ̂ = …% comes free. (Unbiasedness has no such property — E[√X] ≠ √E[X]. Hold that thought for the twist.)

The precise statement

Let x₁,…,xₙ be i.i.d. from f(x;θ₀) with θ₀ interior to the parameter set and f regular: support free of θ, ℓ twice continuously differentiable, I(θ₀) finite and positive definite. Then the MLE θ̂ₙ is consistent (θ̂ₙ → θ₀), invariant (the MLE of g(θ) is g(θ̂ₙ) for any g), and asymptotically normal: √n(θ̂ₙ − θ₀) →ᵈ 𝒩(0, I₁(θ₀)⁻¹), attaining the Cramér–Rao bound in the limit — no regular estimator does better asymptotically. Finite-sample unbiasedness is not among the guarantees: for the Gaussian, E[σ̂²] = ((n−1)/n)·σ².

The profile likelihood — an honest interval for σ

The μ-score's root was x̄ no matter what σ is, so maximising over μ at each fixed σ costs nothing: the profile log-likelihood of σ is the mountain's ridge-line seen from the σ axis. From it you cut an interval in the spirit of Lesson 1's — but read directly off the likelihood, with no need to pretend σ̂'s sampling distribution is exactly normal:

  • the profile — the best log-likelihood any μ can achieve at that σ. With k nuisance parameters you maximise them all out; the χ²₁ calibration below survives.
  • chi-square(1) quantile: 3.84 at 95%, so the cut sits 3.84/2 = 1.92 below the peak. Wilks (1938): 2[ℓp(σ̂) − ℓp(σ₀)] →ᵈ χ²₁ under H₀ — the likelihood-ratio test, folded into an interval. The ½ is because the statistic carries a 2.
  • normal quantile of the Wald recipe (1.96 at 95%) — a parabola fitted at the summit. 1.96² = 3.84: the two intervals coincide exactly when the profile is exactly quadratic, and only then.

The twist — Wald and the likelihood ratio are not the same god either

The Wald interval is a parabola fitted at the summit, symmetric by decree — and not reparameterization-invariant. Build it for σ² and square-root the endpoints: this year's data give …. Build it directly for σ: …. Same data, same 95%, different intervals — the quadratic approximation was taken in different coordinates. The likelihood-ratio interval has no such wobble: the likelihood itself transforms exactly (invariance again), so profiling σ or σ² lands on the same interval, and on skewed profiles it is honestly asymmetric where Wald is blindly symmetric. Parting teaser: on real index returns a Student-t likelihood beats this Gaussian decisively — fat tails are not a correction to the model, they are a different likelihood, and maximum likelihood itself is the referee of that contest.

Why this matters · volarixs

The regime HMM is this lesson at industrial scale: it is fit by maximum likelihood, and EM is nothing but a reliable way to climb exactly this log-likelihood — each E-step builds a lower bound on ℓ, each M-step solves score equations in closed form (regime means and variances with the same ÷n structure, weighted by regime responsibilities). When the fit converges, the thing that has converged is ℓmax.

And the 1.92-drop logic is the honest referee for model choice: a second regime always lifts ℓmax, so the question is whether the lift beats likelihood-ratio noise — the same Δℓ yardstick the profile interval used (with the standard caveat that at the one-regime boundary the χ² calibration needs adjusting). A regime you cannot defend on Δℓ is a regime fitted to noise.
