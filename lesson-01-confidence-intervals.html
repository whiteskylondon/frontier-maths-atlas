What a confidence interval actually claims

The edge is fixed. The interval is random. And one year of backtest usually cannot tell a real +5 bp edge from zero.

Level US Undergraduate (intro→intermediate) FR Licence L2–L3

We are going to run inference on a trading strategy's edge — its average daily profit. To learn what a confidence interval really promises, we cheat: we build the world ourselves. In our simulated market the strategy's true edge is μ = +5 bp per day and the day-to-day noise around it is σ = 100 bp, known exactly. Each "backtest" samples n days from that world and turns them into one interval for the edge. Watch the language: the edge is a fixed number that never moves; the interval is the random object. So whatever "95% confidence" means, it must be a grade for the interval-making procedure, never for any single computed answer.

The words, in plain English

Basis point (bp). One hundredth of one percent. An edge of +5 bp/day sums to 5 × 252 = 1260 bp ≈ 12.6% over a trading year, an annualized Sharpe ratio near 0.8 — a genuinely good strategy. But the daily noise, σ = 100 bp, is 20× the daily signal.

Backtest, sample mean. Run the strategy over n historical days and average the daily PnLs. That average, x̄, is the measured edge — an estimate. A different window of days gives a different x̄: the estimate is the thing that varies.

Standard error. How much x̄ typically wobbles from backtest to backtest: σ/√n. More days shrink it — but only at the √n rate.

Confidence interval, coverage. A recipe that converts one backtest into a range, "x̄ ± some slack", engineered so that across many repetitions a known fraction of the ranges — say 95% — trap the true edge. That long-run success rate is called coverage, and it grades the recipe, not any one range.

Critical value. The slack, counted in standard errors: 1.96 of them buys 95% coverage. Where 1.96 comes from is the derivation below — no theorem needed yet, just those five words.

The formula
  • the sample mean of the n daily PnLs — the measured edge. The only random quantity in the formula: a fresh backtest window moves it.
  • the true edge, fixed and unknown (here +5 bp, because we built the world). The interval hunts it; it never moves.
  • the daily volatility of strategy PnL, assumed known: 100 bp. Estimating it instead swaps the normal for Student's t — question 3 below, and Lesson 2's n−1 story.
  • the standard error — the SD of x̄ across backtests. Precision improves only as √n: halving the interval costs 4× the days.
  • the critical value of the standard normal: 95% → 1.96, 99% → 2.576. How many standard errors of slack the level demands.
  • the confidence level — the long-run fraction of intervals that cover μ. Not the probability that this particular interval succeeded.

The punchline — the edge is smaller than the error bar

Put the pieces together for one trading year, n = 250 days. The standard error is 100/√250 ≈ bp, so the 95% half-width is 1.96 × 100/√250 ≈ bp — nearly 2.5× the +5 bp edge itself. The consequence is the amber stat above: about % of one-year backtests of this genuinely profitable strategy produce an interval containing zero. They cannot reject "no edge at all".

To shrink the half-width to ±5 bp you need 1.96 · 100/√n ≤ 5, i.e. n ≥ (1.96 · 100/5)² ≈ days — about years of daily history. And even that is only half a victory: an interval exactly as wide as the effect turns "reject zero" into a coin flip (slide n to and watch the contain-zero stat hover near 50%). To reject a zero edge 80% of the time takes roughly days ≈ years. Statistical significance on small edges is bought in years, not months.

Everything above compresses into one careful statement. Note where the probability lives — in the procedure — and the duality with hypothesis testing, which question 4 will use.

The precise statement

Let X₁,…,Xₙ be i.i.d. N(μ, σ²) with σ known. The random interval x̄ ± zα/2·σ/√n satisfies P( L(X) ≤ μ ≤ U(X) ) = 1−α, the probability taken over the sampling of the data with μ fixed. Once the data are observed the interval is a constant set: it either contains μ or it does not, and no non-trivial probability attaches to that event. The level 1−α is the procedure's long-run coverage frequency. Moreover the interval is dual to testing: it contains exactly those values μ₀ that the two-sided level-α test of H₀: μ = μ₀ fails to reject.

The twist — the i.i.d. assumption is the biggest lie in the formula

Every formula on this page assumed the n daily PnLs are independent draws. In markets that is false in a specific, dangerous direction: returns autocorrelate (trends, mean-reversion) and volatility clusters. Positive dependence means each new day carries less fresh information than the i.i.d. count pretends, and the variance of x̄ picks up all the covariance cross-terms:

  • the lag-k autocorrelation of daily PnL. Zero under i.i.d.; positive in trending or vol-clustered stretches.
  • the long-run variance ratio. >1 under positive dependence — the honest SE is wider than σ/√n by its square root.
  • the effective sample size — what your n is honestly worth. A 10-year backtest of autocorrelated PnL contains fewer than 10 years of independent evidence.

Even a modest lag-1 autocorrelation ρ₁ = 0.1 (all others ≈ 0) makes the factor 1 + 2(0.1) = 1.2: the honest SE is √1.2 ≈ 1.10× wider and n_eff ≈ n/1.2 ≈ 0.83n. Keep using the i.i.d. band anyway and its true coverage is about % instead of 95% — too narrow exactly in the trending, clustered stretches where a backtest is most likely to fool you.

Why this matters · volarixs

A classical CI is the parametric ancestor of the split-conformal guarantee wired in through MAPIE. The CI promises marginal coverage for a fixed parameter: P(μ ∈ [L,U]) = 1−α over the sampling. Split conformal makes the same shape of claim one level up, for a fresh prediction: P(Y ∈ Ĉ(X)) ≥ 1−α, marginal over the exchangeable data — distribution-free, no normality, no known σ, paying only with ≥ in place of =.

The transferable logic: the guarantee lives over the draw, never conditional on the one interval in front of you. And today's twist transfers too — serial dependence is precisely what breaks exchangeability, which is why conformal coverage is worth monitoring per regime (the regime-HMM's cut of the data) rather than trusted as one global number.
