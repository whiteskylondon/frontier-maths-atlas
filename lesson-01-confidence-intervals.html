What a confidence interval actually claims

The parameter is fixed. The interval is what's random. Watch — and derive — the difference.

Level US Undergraduate (intro→intermediate) FR Licence L2–L3

We know a truth the analyst doesn't: the population mean is μ = 50, with known σ = 12. Each sample of size n yields one interval. Any single interval either covers μ or it doesn't — no probability survives the computation. The confidence level lives entirely in the procedure.

The formula
  • the sample mean, our point estimate of μ. It is random — a fresh sample moves it. This is the only quantity in the formula that changes from draw to draw.
  • the population standard deviation, here assumed known. In practice you estimate it, which swaps the normal for a t-distribution — question 3 below.
  • the standard error — the SD of the sampling distribution of x̄. Precision improves only as √n, so halving the interval takes 4× the data.
  • the critical value from the standard normal. 95% → 1.96, 99% → 2.576. It sets how many standard errors wide the interval is.
  • the confidence level — the long-run fraction of intervals that cover μ. Not the probability for any one interval.

The precise statement

A 1−α confidence procedure produces a random interval [L(X), U(X)] with P( L(X) ≤ μ ≤ U(X) ) = 1−α, the probability taken over the sampling of the data with μ fixed. It describes the method's long-run behaviour, never a single computed interval.

Why this matters · volarixs

This is the parametric ancestor of the conformal guarantee you're wiring in through MAPIE. A classical CI gives marginal coverage for a fixed parameter μ: P(μ ∈ [L,U]) = 1−α over the sampling. Split conformal gives the same shape one level up, for a fresh prediction: P(Y ∈ Ĉ(X)) ≥ 1−α, marginal over the exchangeable data — but distribution-free and with no "σ known" assumption.

Same logic in both: the guarantee is over the draw, not conditional on the specific point — which is exactly the marginal-vs-conditional distinction you already nailed on the placement quiz. The CI assumes normality and known σ to get an exact 1−α; conformal drops both and pays with ≥ instead of =. Seeing them as one claim at two rigour levels is the transferable idea.
