Matrix fundamentals: inner products, XᵀX, and eigenvectors

The bedrock under Lesson 5 — the three primitives every matrix method is built from.

Level US Undergraduate · linear-algebra core FR Licence L1–L2 · prépa MPSI

Three ideas carry almost all of applied linear algebra: the inner product (how aligned two vectors are), the Gram matrix XᵀX (a whole table of inner products), and eigenvectors (the directions a matrix only stretches). Get these solid and the rest — regression, PCA, covariance — stops being formula-memorising and becomes geometry. Both panels below are draggable; move things and watch the numbers.

1 · The inner product

It measures alignment. Zero means orthogonal (perpendicular). The projection of u onto v — the shadow u casts on v's line — is:

  • inner (dot) product. Positive = same general direction, negative = opposing, zero = orthogonal.
  • norm (length) = √⟨u,u⟩.
  • the alignment, normalised to [−1,1]. This is exactly a correlation when u, v are centred.

3 · Eigenvectors & eigenvalues

Most vectors get rotated when you apply A. A few special ones don't — they come out pointing the same way, just longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors; the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. Symmetric matrices (like XᵀX) are the well-behaved case:

  • the eigen-equation. v is a fixed direction of A; λ says how much it stretches (λ<0 flips it, |λ|<1 shrinks it).
  • spectral decomposition of a symmetric A. Q's columns are the orthonormal eigenvectors; Λ is the diagonal of eigenvalues.
  • the condition number. Lesson 5's collinearity gauge — a big ratio means one direction is nearly flat.

The precise statement

(Spectral theorem.) Every real symmetric matrix A has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so A = QΛQᵀ with Q orthogonal. XᵀX is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so all its eigenvalues are real and ≥ 0; its eigenvectors are the orthogonal principal axes, and the eigenvalues are the squared stretch factors along them.

The twist — symmetry is what makes it clean

Drop symmetry and the guarantees go. A pure rotation has complex eigenvalues (no real direction is left unrotated). A shear like [[1,1],[0,1]] is defective — a single repeated eigenvalue with only one eigenvector, so it has no eigenbasis at all. General matrices can have eigenvectors that aren't orthogonal. This is exactly why XᵀX shows up everywhere instead of X: forming XᵀX manufactures a symmetric, real-spectrum, orthogonal-eigenvector object out of any X. The tool that tames the non-symmetric general case is the SVD, which is eigen-decomposition of XᵀX and XXᵀ stitched together — the natural next lesson.

Why this matters · volarixs

Eigen-decomposing a covariance matrix (a centred XᵀX) is PCA: the eigenvectors are your principal risk factors / eigen-portfolios, and each eigenvalue is the variance that factor carries. The top eigenvector is the dominant co-movement mode of your universe; the tiny eigenvalues are the near-collinear directions that made loadings unstable in Lesson 5 — κ = λ_max/λ_min is that instability in one number, computable on any factor panel.

And the inner product is the quiet workhorse underneath: a stock's factor exposure is an inner product of its characteristics with a factor definition; a correlation is a normalised inner product. Getting these two primitives reflexive is what makes the covariance, regression and PCA machinery read as one idea instead of three.
