Matrix fundamentals: inner products, XᵀX, and eigenvectors

Return histories are arrows, correlation is an angle, and a covariance matrix has directions it only stretches.

Level US Undergraduate core FR prépa MPSI · L1

Plain words before any formalism. A vector is a list of numbers read as one arrow — here, an asset's last 60 daily returns is one arrow in a 60-dimensional space. The inner product of two arrows is "multiply day by day, then add": one number measuring how much they moved together. Divide by the two lengths and you get a cosine — and for return series that cosine is the correlation. A matrix is a machine that mixes columns; an eigenvector is a direction that machine only stretches (never turns), and the eigenvalue is the stretch factor. This lesson makes those five words load-bearing: they are the objects Lessons 4–5 quietly used, and Lessons 7–8 run on.

1 · Return histories are vectors; the inner product measures alignment

Take two assets and stack each one's 60 daily returns (in %) into a vector, then center each — subtract its own mean, so only the wiggles remain. Call the centered vectors x and y. The inner product adds up the day-by-day products; the norm ‖x‖ is the arrow's length; and the ratio of the two defines an angle:

Now the finance identity that anchors everything below. For centered series, the inner product is (n−1) times the sample covariance, and normalizing by lengths turns it into the sample correlation — so correlation is literally the cosine of the angle between the two histories:

  • inner (dot) product — multiply day by day, add up. Positive = moved together, negative = opposed, zero = orthogonal (perpendicular). It never exceeds ‖x‖‖y‖ in size — alignment can't beat length — so the ratio is a legal cosine (that fact has a name: the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality).
  • norm (length) = √⟨x,x⟩. For a centered return series, ‖x‖ = √(n−1) × its daily volatility — an arrow's length is its scaled vol.
  • the angle between the two 60-day histories, measured in ℝ⁶⁰. Two vectors always span an ordinary 2D plane, however high the ambient dimension — so θ is a real, drawable angle.
  • the same n−1 from Lesson 2's variance estimator. With n = 60 days, xᵀy = 59 × the sample covariance; the 59 disappears in the correlation because it hits both numerator and denominator.

3 · Matrices mix columns; XᵀX is a table of inner products

Stack p asset histories as the columns of a matrix X (n days × p assets). The most useful way to read a matrix–vector product is column-wise: Xw takes w₁ of column 1, plus w₂ of column 2, and so on. If w is a vector of portfolio weights, Xw is the portfolio's own 60-day return history — one new vector in ℝ⁶⁰. And multiplying X by its own transpose tabulates every pairwise inner product at once:

Divide by n−1 and the Gram matrix XᵀX becomes the covariance matrix Σ. Feed Σ a weight vector on both sides and out comes a single number — the quadratic form — and that number is not an abstraction: it is the variance of the portfolio's return. That is the entire reason Σ can never produce a negative value of wᵀΣw:

  • matrix–vector product, read as a recipe: mix X's columns with weights w. Columns = asset histories, w = the book, Xw = the book's history. This column-wise read is the one that survives into deep learning.
  • the Gram matrix — all pairwise inner products. Diagonal = squared lengths (vols), off-diagonal = alignments. This is Lesson 5's XᵀX, and Lesson 7's Cross-Matrix is this object wearing market clothes.
  • the quadratic form — portfolio variance. ≥ 0 for every w, which is what "positive semidefinite (PSD)" means. The bowl shape this creates is the convexity seed Lesson 14 grows into optimization.
4 · Eigenvectors — the directions a matrix only stretches

Apply Σ to most vectors and the output points somewhere new: the matrix rotates as well as scales. But a few special directions come out parallel to where they went in — only longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors; the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. For a 2×2 matrix you can write both eigenvalues in closed form from just the trace (sum of the diagonal) and the determinant:

  • the eigen-equation: v's direction survives Σ, stretched by λ. λ < 0 would flip it and |λ| < 1 shrinks it — though a covariance matrix, being PSD, only has λ ≥ 0.
  • trace = σ₁² + σ₂², the total variance. λ₊ + λ₋ = tr Σ always: eigen-analysis never creates or destroys variance, it only redistributes it between directions.
  • determinant = λ₊ · λ₋. For a 2-asset covariance, det Σ = σ₁²σ₂²(1−ρ²) → 0 as |ρ| → 1: perfect correlation flattens the ellipse to a line — one direction carries no variance at all.

What you just watched holds for every symmetric matrix, not just the ones the sliders can build: the extreme directions are always perpendicular, the extremes of the quadratic form are always the eigenvalues, and there are always enough eigenvectors to form a full basis. That package of guarantees is the structural theorem of this whole wing:

The precise statement

(Spectral theorem.) Let A be a real symmetric n×n matrix. Then every eigenvalue λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λₙ of A is real, and there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors q₁, …, qₙ, so that A = QΛQᵀ with Q orthogonal (QᵀQ = I) and Λ = diag(λ₁, …, λₙ). If A is a covariance matrix it is in addition positive semidefinite, so every λᵢ ≥ 0. The quadratic form is trapped between the extreme eigenvalues, λₙ ≤ wᵀAw / wᵀw ≤ λ₁, with equality exactly at the corresponding eigenvectors — the extreme directions of the ellipse above.

The twist — every covariance matrix is a rotated diagonal

Read Σ = QΛQᵀ from right to left. Rotate your assets into the eigenbasis (multiply returns by Qᵀ) and the rotated risks are uncorrelated, with variances λ₁, λ₂, … The correlation was never "in" the assets — it was in the coordinate system. Correlated risks are independent risks viewed in a rotated basis, and diversification is a change of basis. PSD gets a geometric read too: the surface w ↦ wᵀΣw is a bowl with elliptical cross-sections — never a saddle. If it dipped below zero anywhere, you would have constructed a portfolio whose return has negative variance, a random variable more certain than certainty. Σ ⪰ 0 is probability refusing to be absurd; Lesson 14 grows this bowl into the whole theory of convex optimization.

Why this matters · volarixs

The Cross-Matrix is a Gram matrix in disguise: every cell is an inner product of two centered series, so every cell is ‖·‖‖·‖cos θ — a table of angles between market histories. pca_factors is this lesson's spectral theorem run on that table: Q's columns are the eigen-portfolios, Λ's diagonal is the variance each one carries, and the top eigenvector is the dominant co-movement mode of the universe.

And sign_stability of a cell is exactly the stability of a cosine under resampling: a near-orthogonal pair (cos θ ≈ 0) has an angle that wobbles across 90°, so its sign flips from resample to resample — the geometry says in advance which cells of the matrix deserve trust.
