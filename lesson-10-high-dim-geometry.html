Lesson 10 · Wing I — the linear-algebra substrate

Geometry of high-dimensional embeddings

Random vectors are strangers and random points share one radius — the curse of dimensionality and the budget behind superposition are the same fact.

LevelUS GraduateFR M2 · ENSAE 3A

Put two portfolio managers in separate rooms and let each build a random long/short book over the same 500 assets. How much will the two books overlap? Plain words before any formalism. An embedding is a list of d numbers used as coordinates — a book's weight on each asset, a token's vector inside a transformer; the dimension d is how long the list is. A vector's norm is its overall size (a book's gross exposure); a unit vector is the vector rescaled to size 1 — pure direction. The cosine similarity of two vectors is their overlap once size is stripped away: +1 means the same book, −1 the opposite book, and 0 means orthogonal — perpendicular, geometry's word for "unrelated". This lesson is one lawful surprise, concentration of measure: as d grows, random cosines stop spreading out and pile up hard at 0, and random lengths pile onto a single value — a thin shell. The two managers' books are all but guaranteed to be strangers. Read one way, that is the curse of dimensionality; read the other, it is the budget that lets 4,096 dimensions carry hundreds of millions of features — and a 500-asset market carry far more than 500 uncrowded strategies.

1 · Two random books are strangers

Scale both books to unit vectors u and v; their cosine is the inner product ⟨u,v⟩ (Lesson 6). The whole derivation fits in one line. A uniformly random direction has no preferred orientation, so rotate coordinates until u lies along the first axis — then the cosine is simply v's first coordinate. Its mean is 0 by sign symmetry, and its variance is a budget argument: the d squared coordinates of a unit vector must add up to exactly 1, and no coordinate is special, so each one gets 1/d in expectation.

  • the first coordinate of a random unit direction — after the rotation, the entire cosine. Rotating is legal because the uniform law on the sphere is rotation-invariant; "fix one vector, randomize the other" is the workhorse trick of high-dimensional probability.
  • the variance — exact at every d, not an asymptotic. The coordinates split the budget Σvᵢ² = 1 equally; nothing deeper about the sphere is needed.
  • the large-d shape: v₁ = z₁/‖z‖ for a Gaussian z, and ‖z‖ concentrates (Section 4), so v₁ inherits z₁'s bell curve. Exact at no finite d — at d = 2 the true law is the arcsine shape you will see below.
  • the spread of overlaps. In a 500-asset universe, 1/√500 ≈ 0.045: two random books typically overlap by ±4.5%, not 0. At d = 1024 the std is 1/32 ≈ 3% — essentially everything is orthogonal to everything.
2 · The same theorem in market clothes — correlation noise

Lesson 6 proved the identity: a sample correlation over n days is a cosine — demean each return series and ρ̂ is the cosine of the angle between two vectors in ℝⁿ. Now run this lesson's theorem in the other direction. If the two series are genuinely independent, their demeaned directions are uniformly random — so their cosine concentrates, with the number of days playing the role of the dimension. Dimension is whatever you have many of: assets in Section 1, days here.

  • the two demeaned n-day return histories, read as vectors (Lesson 6's identity). Demeaning projects out the all-ones direction, so the vectors really live in an (n−1)-dimensional subspace — the same lost dimension as Lesson 2's n−1 divisor.
  • Section 1's cosine with the roles flipped: days are the coordinates. One theorem, two market readings — book overlap across assets, correlation noise across days.
  • the exact null std — for independent Gaussian series, Var(ρ̂) = 1/(n−1), by the identical budget argument in dimension n−1. At n = 60: ≈ 0.13. Two stds ≈ 0.26 — the noise floor a 60-day correlation must clear before its sign deserves any trust.

So a 60-day correlation of 0.2 between two unrelated series is not evidence — it is weather: |ρ̂| up to about 2/√60 ≈ 0.26 appears by chance alone. And a 500-asset correlation matrix has 124,750 cells in which to go fishing, so its extreme entries are guaranteed to look impressive in pure noise. That 0.26 line is precisely what a stability flag has to beat — the volarixs panel below.

4 · Lengths concentrate — the thin shell

Cosines were the angles; now the lengths. Scale a d-dimensional standard Gaussian by 1/√d — coordinates of size N(0, 1/d), the natural units of an embedding — so the expected squared length is exactly 1. The squared norm is then an average of d independent squared normals, and averages of many independent terms barely fluctuate. Lesson 2 met this exact object: Var(s²) = 2σ⁴/(n−1) was the same "variance of a χ² is twice its degrees of freedom" fact wearing estimator clothes.

  • squared standard normal coordinates — each has mean 1, variance 2. That variance-2 is why every constant in this business carries a 2.
  • a mean of d of them — a chi-square over its degrees of freedom. Lesson 2's Var(s²) and this shell are the same computation with different nouns.
  • the norm's fluctuation — the delta method: taking a square root halves relative error, √(2/d)/2 = 1/√(2d). Identical to Lesson 3's Fisher bound for a volatility, std(σ̂) ≈ σ/√(2d): measuring a high-d radius and estimating a vol from d returns are the same estimation problem.
  • a squared distance, expanded into two norms and a cosine. Both norms pin to 1 (this section) and the cross-term pins to 0 (Section 1), so every distance pins to √2 ≈ 1.414 — no free parameters left.

The shell paradox: the Gaussian density is largest at the origin, yet nobody lives there. The volume of a radius-r shell grows like r^{d−1}, and density × volume peaks sharply at radius 1. In d = 1024 the origin is simultaneously the mode of the density and a probability desert.

6 · The blessing — Johnson–Lindenstrauss

Everything so far says high-dimensional geometry is rigid: random distances are all alike. Johnson–Lindenstrauss turns that rigidity into a compression theorem. Project m points onto a random k-dimensional subspace — a k×N Gaussian matrix that has never seen the data. Each squared distance becomes a χ²ₖ-style average that concentrates around its true value with relative error ~1/√k: Section 4's story again, with k in the role of d. Make k just large enough that the error beats ε simultaneously for all m(m−1)/2 pairs — a union bound, which is where the log m comes from — and every distance survives to within 1 ± ε.

  • how many points — entering only through ln m. A million points cost ln 10⁶ ≈ 13.8; doubling the data adds a constant, not a factor.
  • the distortion you tolerate on squared distances. The ε² in the denominator is the real price: halving the tolerance quadruples the dimension.
  • the target dimension: for m = 10⁶ points at ε = 0.1, k ≥ 4 ln(10⁶)/(0.005 − 0.000333…) ≈ 11,842. The ambient dimension N appears nowhere — points from ℝ^(10⁴) or ℝ^(10⁹) compress identically.
  • the random linear map f(x) = Ax/√k, A a k×N matrix of i.i.d. N(0,1) entries. Oblivious: drawn before ever looking at the data. No training, no fitting — randomness alone preserves geometry.

The precise statement

(Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, Dasgupta–Gupta form.) Fix 0 < ε < 1 and any m points x₁, …, x_m ∈ ℝᴺ. Let k ≥ 4 ln m / (ε²/2 − ε³/3), and let f(x) = Ax/√k where A is a k×N matrix with independent N(0,1) entries. Then with probability at least 1/m over the draw of A, (1−ε)‖xᵢ−xⱼ‖² ≤ ‖f(xᵢ)−f(xⱼ)‖² ≤ (1+ε)‖xᵢ−xⱼ‖² holds for every pair i, j simultaneously — in particular such an embedding exists, and redrawing A a few times (or padding k by a constant factor) makes success overwhelmingly likely. The map is linear, oblivious to the data, and k is independent of the ambient dimension N.

The twist — exact orthogonality costs d; near-orthogonality is exponentially cheap

ℝᵈ holds exactly d mutually perpendicular directions — a hard budget, and the reason "one feature per neuron" would cap a network at d features. Relax perpendicular to almost perpendicular, pairwise |cos| ≤ ε, and the budget explodes: Section 1 says a random pair violates ε-orthogonality with probability ≈ 2e^(−dε²/2), so on the order of e^(ε²d/2) random directions can coexist, all pairwise ε-orthogonal — exponential in d. At ε = 0.1 inside a 4,096-dimensional residual stream that is e^(20.48) ≈ 8×10⁸ almost-independent directions: hundreds of millions of feature slots in a space with only 4,096 exact ones. That surplus is the geometric bank account superposition (Lesson 20) spends — a model stores far more features than neurons by accepting ε of crosstalk. The market version: a 500-asset universe caps you at 500 exactly-uncorrelated books, but at pairwise |ρ| ≤ 0.2 the same count allows ~e^(10) ≈ 22,000. Crowding doesn't come from running out of orthogonal space — it comes from everyone's "random-looking" books not actually being random.

Why this matters · volarixs

sign_stability is a war fought against Section 2's noise floor. A Cross-Matrix cell's sample ρ on a 60-day window carries sampling std ≈ 1/√60 ≈ 0.13 even when the true relationship is zero — so any cell with |ρ| below about 2/√n ≈ 0.26 has a sign that flips from resample to resample. The flag isn't measuring vague "reliability"; it is measuring whether a cosine clears the 1/√n geometry of this lesson.

pca_factors survives the same noise for the opposite reason: concentration works for it. A factor direction aggregates thousands of noisy pairwise cosines at once, and averaging concentrates — which is why the dominant eigen-portfolio stays stable across windows even when most individual cells' signs do not.
