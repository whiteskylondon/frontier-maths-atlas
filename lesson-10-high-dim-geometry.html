Geometry of high-dimensional embeddings

In high dimensions everything is far, everything is orthogonal, and that turns out to be a gift.

LevelUS GraduateFR Master M2 · ENSAE 3A

Every token, every feature vector in a modern model is a point in a space of hundreds or thousands of dimensions. Our 3-D intuition breaks there — and the way it breaks is lawful. Random vectors become nearly orthogonal, distances concentrate so that near and far blur together, and a Gaussian's mass drifts off the centre onto a thin shell. These aren't pathologies to avoid; they're the reason embeddings, cosine similarity, and superposition work at all.

The central fact — concentration

Take two independent random unit vectors in ℝ^d. Their cosine similarity is their dot product, and it behaves like a mean-zero Gaussian whose spread shrinks with dimension:

So the typical angle between two random vectors rushes toward 90° as d grows — a standard deviation of only 1/√d. In 1000 dimensions almost every pair of directions is essentially orthogonal. The companion fact for lengths: a standard Gaussian's norm concentrates tightly at √d.

  • cosine similarity of two unit vectors. The survivor metric in high-d — angles stay meaningful where raw distances stop being.
  • the variance of that cosine. Concentration rate: double the dimension, tighten the spread by √2.
  • the shell radius. Where a high-d Gaussian actually lives — not near its own mode at the origin.

The blessing — Johnson–Lindenstrauss

Concentration cuts the other way too. Because random projections preserve geometry with high probability, you can crush n points from any dimension down to just O(log n / ε²) dimensions and keep every pairwise distance within a (1 ± ε) factor:

The target size depends on the number of points and the tolerance — not on the original dimension. That's why approximate nearest-neighbour search, random-feature methods, and sketching all work: high-d structure is compressible precisely because it concentrates.

The precise statement

For u, v drawn uniformly on the unit sphere Sᵈ⁻¹, ⟨u,v⟩ has mean 0 and variance 1/d, and concentrates as 𝒩(0, 1/d) for large d — so random directions are asymptotically orthogonal. A standard Gaussian x ∈ ℝᵈ has ‖x‖ concentrated at √d with fluctuations O(1): ℙ(|‖x‖ − √d| ≥ t) ≤ 2e^{−ct²}. Johnson–Lindenstrauss: any n points admit a linear map into k = O(ε⁻²log n) dimensions preserving all pairwise squared distances within (1 ± ε), with high probability, via a random projection.

The twist — the curse and the blessing are one fact

Concentration is usually taught as the "curse of dimensionality": when all pairwise distances converge, the nearest neighbour is barely closer than the farthest, so distance-based methods (kNN, k-means, radius queries) lose their grip — the ratio of nearest to farthest distance → 1. But the identical phenomenon is the blessing: near-orthogonality means you can pack an exponential number of almost-orthogonal directions into d dimensions (there are ~e^{cd} vectors pairwise within a few degrees of orthogonal), which is exactly how a network stores far more features than it has neurons — superposition (Lesson 20). And it's why cosine, not Euclidean distance, is the right embedding metric: angles carry the signal that lengths have washed out. Same maths, read as a warning or a superpower depending on whether you cluster by distance or separate by direction.

Why this matters · volarixs

The moment you embed cells or assets as vectors and search by similarity — the pgvector cell_card similarity your design doc floats ("cells like dispersion → skew"), or clustering return-vectors of a large universe — you're in this regime. Use cosine, not raw Euclidean distance, and expect naive nearest-neighbour to degrade as the vector dimension climbs: that's concentration, not a bug in the index.

It also sharpens a Lesson-8 point: a high-dimensional factor space where "everything looks equidistant" is often a low-rank object in disguise — reduce first (SVD/PCA), then measure similarity in the compressed space where distances still mean something. And Johnson–Lindenstrauss is the license to do that fast: random-project to O(log n) dims for approximate similarity at scale.
