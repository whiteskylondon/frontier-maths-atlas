Lesson 24 · Wing V — training-dynamics mysteries

Emergence as phase transitions

A capability that is absent at 1010 parameters and present at 1011 — or a ruler with no gradations below its own smallest one.

Level US Graduate FR Master 2 – PhD

Plot exact-match accuracy on multi-digit arithmetic against model size and you see a flat line at zero across two orders of magnitude, and then a rise to something you would call working. Wei et al. (2022) named this pattern an emergent ability: absent in small models, present in large ones, and not extrapolable from the small ones. Taken at face value it is the most consequential claim in the field, because it says you cannot know what your next run can do until you have paid for it — and Lesson 21's smooth, boring, forecastable loss curve would tell you nothing about capability. This lesson asks the only question a plot like that can settle: is the jump in the model, or in the ruler? Two lines of probability, one about composing a metric and one about counting a test set, do most of the work. They do not do all of it, and being exact about the remainder is the point.

The words, before the machinery
  • emergent abilityWei et al.'s definition: a capability not present in smaller models and present in larger ones, whose appearance could not have been predicted by extrapolating the smaller ones. Note that the definition is about a measured curve, not about the network. That gap is the whole lesson.
  • metricthe function turning a model's output into a score. Exact match (also called exact string match) gives 1 only if every token of the answer is right; multiple-choice grade gives 1 only if the argmax option is right. Both are thresholded: they throw away how close you were. Schaeffer et al. observed that the emergent-looking tasks in BIG-Bench cluster overwhelmingly on this small family of metrics.
  • p, per-token successthe probability the model produces the correct token at a given position — the quantity that actually improves with scale. Written p(N) with N the parameter count. We will always plot against x = log10N, because that is the axis every scaling plot uses.
  • Lthe number of tokens that must all be right for the answer to count. Five-digit addition needs ~6; a proof step needs dozens. L is a property of the task and of your answer format, not of the model.
  • continuous metricone that moves a little when the output gets a little better: per-token log-loss in nats (Lesson 15's cross-entropy, the same units as Lesson 21's scaling curves), or 1 − normalized edit distance, which in expectation is just p. A nat is a natural-log unit of surprise; divide by ln 2 ≈ 0.693 for bits.
  • resolutionthe smallest non-zero score a test set of n items can report: 1/n. With 200 items, nothing below 0.5% exists. A capability with true success rate 10−3 is not "weak" on such a test set; it is unrepresentable.
  • sharpnesshow fast a curve rises, measured here as its maximum slope in accuracy per decade of N. The one number that can be compared between two metrics plotted on the same axis. We will compute it in closed form and it will be less impressive than you expect.
  • phase transitionin physics, a point where a derivative of the free energy fails to exist in the infinite-size limit — a genuine non-analyticity. Nothing in the LLM literature has demonstrated non-analyticity in anything. "Phase transition" is used loosely for "sharp on a log axis", and the loose usage is doing rhetorical work the maths has not paid for.
A metric that multiplies

Suppose the answer needs L tokens and the model gets each one right independently with probability p, which improves smoothly with scale. Take p logistic in x = log10N — a clean stand-in for "rises steadily, no knee anywhere", with steepness s measured per decade. The exact-match score is then the probability of an unbroken run:

  • the composition: independence turns a per-token probability into a product. Independence is an idealization — real errors are correlated along a sequence, which makes EM higher than pL but does not change the shape argument.
  • the steepness of the underlying improvement, in units of per decade of N. The only thing that could plausibly be called a property of the model. s is what a scaling law (Lesson 21) would pin down; it is a slider here because nobody measures p directly.
  • the scale at which per-token success passes one half. Fixed at 109.5 parameters throughout, so that changing L moves only the metric and never the model.

Differentiate. The chain rule gives the identity the whole debate turns on, and for the logistic p it closes in one line:

  • the general form: no logistic assumed. Read it as: the benchmark's slope is L times the relative rate of improvement of p, weighted by how much of the benchmark is already won. The L in front is why people expect the composed metric to be dramatically sharper.
  • where the benchmark curve is steepest: at per-token accuracy L/(L+1), i.e. at x = x0 + (ln L)/s. For L = 10 that is p = 0.909 — the visible jump happens when the model is already right nine times in ten per token.
  • the maximum slope itself, against s/4 for p alone (a logistic's own maximum). The ratio is 4(L/(L+1))L+1, which increases with L to a hard ceiling of 4/e ≈ 1.4715: 1.402 at L = 10, 1.436 at L = 20, never more. Composition buys under 50% extra steepness — ever.

So the sharpness is not where the illusion lives. Here is where it lives. The benchmark stays under any threshold ε until p has climbed past the L-th root of ε:

  • the hidden fraction: how far the continuous metric has travelled while the benchmark still reads under ε. At L = 10 and ε = 5%, three quarters of the per-token improvement happens below the benchmark's own 5% line. At L = 20 it is 86%. The metric is not sharpening the curve; it is deleting the first three quarters of it.
  • the threshold at which you would say "the model can't do this". On a real benchmark ε is not chosen by you — it is set by the test set's resolution 1/n, which is the second half of this lesson.

The other half — what n items can resolve

The first panel left the benchmark pinned near zero over most of the range. A real benchmark does something stronger: it reports exactly zero, because it has finitely many items. Let the model's true success rate on the task be q — the sequence-level rate, so q = pL if you like, but the argument needs nothing about where q came from. Score n independent items:

  • the true success probability — the thing that exists whether or not you can see it. This is Lesson 1's fixed parameter: the estimate is random, the parameter is not.
  • the measured accuracy, with standard error √(q(1−q)/n) — Lesson 1's binomial noise. At q = 10−3 and n = 200 the standard error is 0.22%, larger than q itself: the estimator's noise dwarfs the quantity it estimates, exactly as ±12.4 bp dwarfed a +5 bp edge there.
  • the probability the benchmark reports a flat zero. Not "approximately zero" — the literal integer 0, which is what "the model cannot do this" looks like in a table. The approximation e−nq says the only thing that matters is the product nq, the expected number of hits.

Invert it. Demand that the capability show up — at least one success — with probability 0.9, and the required test-set size follows immediately, along with the smallest capability a given n can see:

  • items needed to see the capability at least once, 90% of the time. Derivation: (1−q)n ≤ 0.1 ⟺ n ln(1−q) ≤ ln 0.1 ⟺ n ≥ ln(0.1)/ln(1−q), the inequality flipping because ln(1−q) < 0. Then round up.
  • the folk form, ln 10 over q. It overestimates by about q/2 in relative terms, since −ln(1−q) = q + q²/2 + …: under 0.05% error at q = 10−3, about 5% at q = 0.1. Cheap and safe in the regime that matters, which is small q.
  • the apparent threshold: the weakest capability your test set can detect 90% of the time. Everything below q★ reads zero and therefore reads "absent". Scale a model until q crosses q★ and the benchmark switches on — a threshold that lives entirely in n.

The precise statement

(i) Metric composition. Let p(x) ∈ (0,1) be differentiable and increasing in x = log10N, and let L tokens be independent, so that EM(x) = p(x)L. Then d EM/dx = L·pL·d(ln p)/dx exactly. If p is logistic with steepness s, d EM/dx = s L pL(1−p), whose unique stationary point is p⋆ = L/(L+1), i.e. x⋆ = x0 + (ln L)/s, with maximum value s(L/(L+1))L+1. Since (1+1/L)L+1 decreases in L to e, the ratio of that maximum to the maximum slope of p itself (s/4) is 4(L/(L+1))L+1, which increases monotonically in L to 4/e ≈ 1.4715 and never attains it. Composition therefore cannot make the curve 47.2% steeper, and for the L ≤ 20 of the panel above it does not reach 44%. But EM < ε ⟺ p < ε1/L, so the fraction ε1/L of p's range is spent below the visible threshold, and that fraction → 1 as L grows. A sharp-looking benchmark curve is thus produced by a perfectly smooth p through delay and suppression, not through steepening.

(ii) Resolution. If k ~ Binomial(n, q) then Pr[k = 0] = (1−q)n exactly, so a capability with q ≪ 1/n reads as a literal zero with probability ≈ e−nq ≈ 1. Detection with probability ≥ 0.9 requires n ≥ ln(0.1)/ln(1−q) = ln 10/(−ln(1−q)) = (2.3026/q)(1 − q/2 + O(q²)); equivalently, a test set of size n cannot 90%-detect any q below q⋆ = 1 − 0.11/n ≈ 2.3026/n. Since q⋆ depends on n alone, the scale at which a benchmark "switches on" is a property of the evaluation, not of the model.

(iii) Scope. (i) and (ii) show that a sharp benchmark curve is consistent with smooth underlying improvement — so sharpness under a thresholded metric at coarse resolution is not evidence of a discontinuity in the model. Neither result shows the converse: they do not prove that no capability changes sharply with scale, and they say nothing about tasks measured with continuous metrics at adequate resolution.

Re-applied — the backtest that switches on

Both failures are native to performance reporting, and neither needs a language model. Take the composition first. A tear sheet's favourite line is "all 12 months profitable". If m is the probability a given month closes green, that headline is m12 — the same power, for exactly the same reason:

  • the thresholded headline. The underlying skill moves 1.6× (0.50 → 0.80); the reported quantity moves 281× — exactly (8/5)12. Anyone reading the headline across a series of ever-larger capital allocations sees a strategy that "starts working" at some size.
  • the fixscore the continuous thing. Mean monthly return, or the log-loss of the directional call (Lesson 15's strictly proper score), instead of hit-rate-above-a-bar or months-green. The information was always there; the metric threw it away.

Then resolution. Lesson 1's world had a genuine +5 bp/day edge against σ = 100 bp of daily noise, and one year of data gave a 95% half-width of ±12.4 bp — two and a half times the effect. Run that backtest at n = 60, 250, 1 000 and 3 200 days and report the binary "significant positive edge at 5%: yes/no". The probability of a yes is 6%, 12%, 35%, 81% — that is Φ(μ√n/σ − 1.96), where Φ is the standard normal CDF, Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z) for Z ~ N(0,1), the same object that supplied Lesson 1's 1.96. A perfectly smooth function of √n, reported through a threshold. Four data points, and you have manufactured an emergence curve: no, no, no, yes. Nothing switched on. You bought resolution. The discipline is the same in both halves: pick a continuous metric, then size the sample to the effect you are hunting — and if the effect is 10−3, budget the 2 302 trials that implies before concluding it is not there.

The twist — three of the four ingredients are yours

An emergence claim is a claim about the derivative of a curve. But you never had the curve: you had a handful of points on it. Count what produced those points — the metric (you chose exact match), the test-set size (you chose 200 items), the scale spacing (you trained at whatever sizes the budget allowed, typically one point per half-decade), and the model family. Only the last belongs to nature. A derivative estimated from four points, thresholded by a metric of your choosing and floored by a resolution of your choosing, is a statement about your evaluation protocol wearing the costume of a discovery. Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo (2023) made this concrete in the strongest possible way: they induced emergent-looking curves in vision models on tasks nobody calls emergent, purely by swapping in a discontinuous metric, and made the published emergent curves disappear by swapping the metric back.

Second barrel, and it cuts the other way. None of this shows capabilities cannot genuinely jump. Phase transitions are real in physics; and in training dynamics we have at least one well-documented sharp change that is not a metric artifact — the formation of induction heads, which Olsson et al. (2022) found as a visible bump in the loss curve itself, a continuous metric at full resolution. Lesson 23's grokking is another: there the transition is in training time at fixed scale, and the evidence is in the weights — a memorizing solution is replaced by a generalizing circuit — so it is a claim about the network's internals rather than about a benchmark's shape. (Even there, honesty: Nanda et al. showed the circuit forms gradually underneath the jump, so the sharpness of the accuracy curve is again partly the metric's.) The lesson is not "emergence is fake". It is that the burden of proof sits on continuous metrics and adequate resolution — and once you meet that burden, whatever sharpness survives is real and worth studying.

Why this matters · volarixs

sign_stability is a thresholded metric of exactly the kind this lesson is about: the share of subperiods in which a cell's β̂ keeps its sign, gated at some bar. Model a cell as having a true t-ratio t = β/SE(β̂) with independent subperiods; then the share is Φ(t), and the gate is a step applied to it. As data quality improves and t drifts smoothly from 0.5 to 1.5, the underlying share moves 0.691 → 0.933 — perfectly smooth — while a gate at 0.8 flips at the single point t = 0.842. A cell that "comes alive" between two rebuilds may have crossed a threshold you set, not a threshold in the market.

The resolution half is the more expensive one. A Cross-Matrix cell (Lesson 7) conditioned finely enough has few observations, and Lesson 21's panel already priced the split; this lesson adds the sharper version — below a cell's own resolution, a real relationship reports as nothing, and nothing is indistinguishable from absent. Two habits follow: keep and plot the continuous diagnostic (the β̂ and its standard error, the elasticity itself) alongside any gated flag, and record each cell's n so that "no signal here" can be read as "not resolvable at this n". The honest boundary: the Φ(t) model above assumes independent subperiods with a stable β, which is exactly what a regime-conditioned panel violates — treat it as the right shape of argument, not a calibration.
