Convexity, saddle points & the reparameterization trick

Whether a problem has one answer or many is decided by the sign of a curvature matrix.

LevelUS GraduateFR Master M2 · ENSAE 3A

A convex loss is a single bowl: any local minimum is the global one, and descent can't get stuck. Most deep-learning losses are non-convex — riddled with saddle points where the gradient vanishes but you're not at a minimum. What separates a minimum from a maximum from a saddle is the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives: read its eigenvalues and you know the local shape. This lesson also fixes a separate problem — how to take gradients through a random node — with the reparameterization trick, which quant desks already know as the pathwise method for Greeks.

Convexity and the second-order test

At a stationary point (∇f = 0), the Hessian's eigenvalues classify it: all positive → a minimum (bowl up in every direction), all negative → a maximum, mixed signs → a saddle (up some ways, down others).

  • ∇²fthe Hessian: matrix of second partials. Local curvature; its eigenvalues are the curvatures along principal directions (Lesson 6).
  • ⪰ 0positive semidefinite: all eigenvalues ≥ 0. Curves up (or flat) in every direction — the definition of a convex bowl.
  • saddle∇f = 0 but not an extremum. Mixed-sign Hessian; the dominant critical point in high dimensions (Lesson 9).

The precise statement

For C² f, convexity on a domain is equivalent to ∇²f(x) ⪰ 0 everywhere; then every stationary point is a global minimum and the set of minimizers is convex. At a stationary point, the signature of ∇²f classifies it: positive definite → strict local min, negative definite → max, indefinite → saddle. For an objective 𝔼_{z∼p_θ}[f(z)] with a reparameterizable p_θ (z = g(θ, ε), ε ∼ base), ∇_θ 𝔼[f] = 𝔼_ε[∇_θ f(g(θ,ε))] (pathwise) — generally lower variance than the score-function estimator 𝔼[f(z)∇_θ log p_θ(z)], which needs only that p_θ be differentiable in θ but not that f be differentiable.

The twist — non-convex sounds fatal but mostly isn't

Two reassurances that make modern ML work. First, the villain of a non-convex loss is not local minima but saddle points — and in high dimensions a random critical point is overwhelmingly a saddle (to be a minimum, all d Hessian eigenvalues must be positive; with signs roughly independent that's ~2⁻ᵈ likely). Saddles have a downhill direction, so SGD's noise slips off them; the minima it lands in are usually comparably good. Second, the reparameterization vs score-function split is a real, bankable trade you already meet as pathwise vs likelihood-ratio Greeks: pathwise (reparam) exploits the payoff's derivative for low variance but needs smoothness — it struggles exactly at kinks like a digital's delta, where the likelihood-ratio method, which never differentiates the payoff, takes over. The two methods are complementary, and knowing which regime you're in is the whole skill. Convexity buys you a unique answer; when you can't have it, geometry (saddles are escapable) and variance control (pick the right estimator) are what save you.

Why this matters · volarixs

The cleanest convex object you own is the minimum-variance portfolio: variance is wᵀΣw, and because the covariance Σ is positive semidefinite (its eigenvalues ≥ 0, straight from Lessons 6 and 8), the objective is convex — so the optimizer has a unique global solution with no saddles to trap it. Mean-variance optimization is a convex quadratic program for exactly this reason. When Σ is near-singular (collinear assets, tiny eigenvalues), the bowl gets a nearly-flat direction — the same ill-conditioning that shrinkage / ridge (Lessons 5, 8) fixes.

And the reparameterization trick is your Monte-Carlo Greeks: simulate returns as z = μ + σε and the pathwise estimator gives smooth-payoff sensitivities at far lower variance than bumping, while the likelihood-ratio estimator rescues the discontinuous payoffs (digitals, barriers). Same mathematics that lets a VAE (Lesson 28) train by gradient descent.
