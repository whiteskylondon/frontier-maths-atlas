Convexity, saddle points & the reparameterization trick

Whether a problem has one answer or many is decided by the sign of a curvature matrix.

LevelUS GraduateFR M2 · ENSAE 3A

Two ideas run this lesson, and both are promises. A convex function is one shaped like a bowl: pick any two points on its graph and the straight chord between them never dips below the graph. Bowls promise that rolling downhill reaches the bottom, because there is only one. A saddle point is where that promise breaks: the ground is flat — the gradient, the slope vector from Lesson 9, is zero — yet you are at the bottom of nothing, because the surface curves up one way and down another. Which case you are in is read off the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, whose eigenvalues (Lesson 6) are the curvatures along its eigenvector directions. The second promise is that you can differentiate through sampling: when a loss is an average over random draws whose distribution depends on your parameters, there are exactly two honest ways to push the gradient inside the expectation — and a trading desk already knows them as pathwise and likelihood-ratio Greeks.

Convex, in plain words first

The set version first: a set is convex if it has no dents — the straight segment between any two of its points stays inside it. (Portfolio constraints like "weights sum to 1, each between 0 and 1" carve out a convex set.) A function is convex when its graph is bowl-shaped over such a set, which the chord says precisely:

  • blend weight, sliding from y (α=0) to x (α=1). Left side: f at a blend of the inputs. Right side: the same blend of the outputs — a point on the chord.
  • any two points of the (convex) domain. The domain must be convex so the blended point is somewhere f is defined.
  • "the chord sits above the graph". Strict < for α∈(0,1), x≠y is strict convexity — a bowl with no flat patches, hence at most one minimizer.

Two equivalent tests are more usable in practice. The first-order test: the tangent plane at any point is a global under-estimator — a convex function hides nothing below its linearizations. The second-order test reads curvature directly:

  • the gradient — steepest-ascent vector (Lesson 9). If ∇f(x★)=0 the inequality reads f(y) ≥ f(x★) for every y: a stationary point of a convex f is already globally optimal.
  • the Hessian, (∇²f)ᵢⱼ = ∂²f/∂xᵢ∂xⱼ. Symmetric, so it has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (Lesson 6) — the principal curvatures and their directions.
  • positive semidefinite: vᵀ(∇²f)v ≥ 0 for every direction v — curves up or is flat every way you slice it. ≻ 0 (all eigenvalues > 0) gives strict convexity and a unique minimizer.

Why bowls keep their promise, in one line: if x★ is a local minimum but some y beats it, f(y) < f(x★), the chord inequality gives f((1−α)x★+αy) ≤ (1−α)f(x★)+αf(y) < f(x★) for every α∈(0,1) — lower points arbitrarily close to x★, contradicting local minimality. So no better y exists: every local minimum of a convex function is global.

Push the chord to averages and you get Jensen's inequality: for convex f, — the function of the average understates the average of the function. You have already been burned by it: Lesson 2's c₄ story is Jensen with the inequality flipped, because √· is concave — E[√V] ≤ √E[V], so the sample standard deviation is biased low.

And the convexity you already own: portfolio variance. As a function of the weights w it is the quadratic wᵀΣw, whose Hessian is 2Σ — and Lesson 6 proved Σ ⪰ 0 for the deepest possible reason:

  • covariance matrix of the asset returns r. PSD not by assumption but by construction: quadratic forms in it are variances.
  • portfolio weights; wᵀΣw is exactly Var(wᵀr), the portfolio variance (Lesson 6). A quadratic with PSD Hessian is convex — so minimum-variance and mean-variance programs are convex programs.
  • the quadratic form = variance of the return aᵀr — cannot be negative. This one inequality is the entire license behind "the optimizer found the optimal portfolio".

When convexity fails, stationary points stop being conclusions. At any point with ∇f = 0, the Hessian's eigenvalue signs are the whole local story: all positive → a strict minimum; all negative → a maximum; mixed signs → a saddle — uphill along some eigenvectors, downhill along others; an eigenvalue at zero → a flat direction, the degenerate valley Lesson 5 met in collinear regression. Differentiating through randomness — the two identities

Now the second promise. Objectives in VAEs (Lesson 28), policy gradients (Lesson 31), and Monte-Carlo pricing all take the form Ex∼pθ[f(x)] — an average over draws whose distribution depends on the parameters. You cannot backprop through "draw a sample". There are exactly two honest ways to move ∇θ inside the expectation, and everything in this corner of ML and of derivatives pricing is one of the two:

  • the reparameterization: write the sample as x = g(θ,ε), θ entering deterministically. Gaussian case: g = μ + σε. Valid when θ ↦ f(g(θ,ε)) is differentiable for a.e. ε and the derivative is dominated by an integrable bound — that is what licenses swapping ∇ and E.
  • the frozen noise: all the randomness, none of the parameters. Gradients now flow through the deterministic path θ → x → f — this is "the reparameterization trick".
  • the score — Lesson 3's likelihood machinery reappearing. This identity needs only the density differentiable in θ; f may be a black box, a jump, a discrete indicator.
  • both are exact and unbiased where valid. They differ wildly in variance — that is the shootout below.

Markets translation: a Black–Scholes Monte-Carlo path is already a reparameterization — the parameter S₀ enters deterministically and the noise Z is frozen. Differentiate the payoff along the path, or multiply the payoff by the score:

  • terminal price on the path; Z ∼ 𝒩(0,1) is the frozen noise. ∂S_T/∂S₀ = S_T/S₀ — the path derivative every pathwise Greek rides.
  • pathwise call delta: chain rule through the payoff (S_T−K)⁺. Valid because a call is Lipschitz — differentiable except at the single point S_T = K, which paths hit with probability 0.
  • likelihood-ratio delta: payoff Π times the score of the lognormal density in S₀. Never touches Π′ — works for any integrable payoff, including digitals.
  • the closed forms both estimators chase: vanilla Δ = Φ(d₁) ≈ 0.540 here; digital Δ = φ(d₂)/(S₀σ√T) ≈ 0.01985. With S₀ = K = 100, σ = 20%, T = 1, r = 0: d₁ = 0.10, d₂ = −0.10.

The precise statement

Let f be C² on a convex open set. Then f is convex iff ∇²f(x) ⪰ 0 for all x; if ∇²f(x) ≻ 0 for all x, f is strictly convex and any stationary point is the unique global minimizer. At a stationary point of a general C² function, the Hessian's signature classifies it: positive definite → strict local minimum, negative definite → local maximum, mixed signs → saddle. Reparameterization: let x = g(θ,ε), ε ∼ p₀ fixed, so x ∼ p_θ. If θ ↦ f(g(θ,ε)) is differentiable for a.e. ε and ‖∇_θ f(g(θ,ε))‖ ≤ M(ε) with 𝔼[M] < ∞ (domination), then ∇_θ 𝔼_{x∼p_θ}[f(x)] = 𝔼_ε[∇_θ f(g(θ,ε))]. If instead only the density is differentiable in θ (f merely integrable), ∇_θ 𝔼[f] = 𝔼[f(x) ∇_θ ln p_θ(x)].

The twist — the enemy isn't minima, and a failing Greek built REINFORCE

First upgrade: in high dimension, non-convexity's stereotypical villain — the bad local minimum — is mostly a myth. A critical point needs all d Hessian eigenvalues positive to be a minimum; in the random-matrix picture of generic landscapes (Bray–Dean, and Dauphin et al.'s empirics in networks), the signs behave roughly independently near a typical critical point, so all-positive is exponentially rare — order 2⁻ᵈ, about 10⁻³⁰ at d = 100. What you actually hit are saddles, and saddles are escapable: noise walks you off along the negative-curvature eigenvector (your panel above), and perturbed gradient descent provably escapes them. The local minima that survive in overparameterized nets tend to sit at nearly the same loss — non-convexity did not kill deep learning; it replaced "one bowl" with "many good floors" (Wing V makes this quantitative).

Second upgrade: the digital option's dead pathwise Greek is not a pricing curiosity — it is the exact reason score-function gradients exist in ML. Sampling a discrete token is a digital payoff: nudging the logits infinitesimally leaves the sampled outcome unchanged almost everywhere, so the pathwise gradient is 0 — and REINFORCE (Lesson 31) is precisely the likelihood-ratio estimator, variance and all. The reparameterization trick with a continuous relaxation (Gumbel-softmax; Lesson 28's VAEs) is the pathwise cure.

Why this matters · volarixs

Σ ⪰ 0 is the license behind every mean-variance and minimum-variance overlay volarixs runs: wᵀΣw is convex by construction, so "the optimizer found the global optimum" is a theorem, not marketing. The caveat is Lesson 5's: on a collinear returns panel the smallest eigenvalues of Σ — the tail of the pca_factors spectrum — sit near zero, and the bowl degenerates toward a knife-edge valley: the optimum still exists, but its location along the flat directions is numerically meaningless. Shrink or truncate (Lessons 5, 8) before trusting the weights.

And pathwise-vs-likelihood-ratio is the daily estimator choice on an exotic or digital book: smooth payoffs → pathwise deltas and vegas at low variance; digitals and barrier-adjacent payoffs → the payoff's derivative is a lie (zero a.e.), so switch to likelihood-ratio or smooth the payoff. The failure mode isn't an error message — it is a confident zero.
