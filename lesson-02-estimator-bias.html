Estimators, bias, and the n−1 story

Why dividing by n understates realized vol, why n−1 doesn't fully fix it — and why "unbiased" was never the prize.

Level US Advanced undergraduate FR Licence L3 – Master 1 · ENSAE 1A

A vol desk looks back at the last 20 daily returns of an index and quotes one number: the realized volatility — the typical size of a daily move, scaled up to a year. That number comes out of an estimator: a fixed recipe that turns observed data into a guess at something true but hidden. Run the recipe on many different months and the guesses scatter; the shape of that scatter is the sampling distribution, the same object underneath Lesson 1's confidence intervals. If the scatter centres on the truth the recipe is unbiased; if its long-run average misses — every month, in the same direction — that systematic gap is bias. This lesson finds bias hiding inside the most routine calculation in finance, twice: the two classic recipes for variance are both shaded, for two different reasons, and one of the two shavings survives every divisor you throw at it.

The words, in plain English
  • estimatora formula applied to data, written with a hat: σ̂² estimates σ². The recipe is the estimator; the number it prints on one particular window is an estimate.
  • sampling distributionthe spread of the estimator's outputs across many possible data windows. You only ever see one draw from it — this month's number — but its shape governs how much to trust that draw.
  • biasthe gap between the sampling distribution's centre and the truth: E[σ̂²] − σ². Noise averages out; bias doesn't. A biased vol estimator misquotes in the same direction forever.
  • degrees of freedomhow many numbers in the data remain free after the constraints you imposed. Computing the sample mean pins down one linear combination — 20 returns minus 1 constraint leave 19 free deviations.
  • realized volatilitythe standard deviation of recent daily returns, annualized by √252. The desk's backward-looking vol number, as opposed to implied vol, which markets quote forward.
Two recipes for one number

The true world in this lesson: daily index returns with true daily vol σ = 1.2% (σ² = 1.44 %²) and a whisper of drift, ≈ 0.02% a day. The desk sees a window of n = 20 returns, subtracts the sample mean because the true drift μ is unavailable, and divides the sum of squared deviations by… what, exactly? Two candidates:

  • the i-th daily return, in percent. Modelled i.i.d. here; Lesson 7 relaxes that for the Cross-Matrix.
  • the sample mean of the window. Chosen by the data to minimise Σ(rᵢ−c)² over all c — so deviations from r̄ are the smallest they can possibly be, smaller than from the true μ.
  • variance with the ÷n divisor. This is exactly what maximum likelihood hands you for a Gaussian — Lesson 3 derives it — and it is biased low.
  • the Bessel-corrected variance, ÷(n−1). Exactly unbiased for σ² under any distribution with finite variance.
  • the annualization factor: 252 trading days, and variance adds across independent days. Multiplicative — so any bias in the daily number passes straight through to the annual quote.
Why ÷n comes out low — the derivation

The culprit is r̄. It is fitted to this particular window, so it sits closer to these 20 points than the true μ does, and squared deviations measured from it come out too small. Split each deviation through μ — an exact algebraic identity, true for every sample:

  • the true drift — unknown, which is why we subtracted r̄ instead. The identity quantifies the cost of that substitution.
  • the stolen piece: how much spread disappears because r̄ chased the sample. Always ≥ 0 — fitting can only shrink the measured spread, never inflate it.

Take expectations. The first term averages to nσ²; the stolen piece averages to n·Var(r̄) = n·(σ²/n) = σ² — exactly one observation's worth of variance:

So the honest divisor is n−1, and dividing by n falls short by the ratio of the two:

At n = 20 — one trading month — the factor is exactly (n−1)/n = 0.95: a built-in 5% shading of variance. At n = 2 it is ½: the ÷n recipe expects half the true variance. The gap fades as n grows; both estimators are consistent.

Everything so far used no normality — only i.i.d. draws with a finite variance. Normality enters only when you want the exact shape of the sampling distribution you just watched, and the exact size of the second bias below.

The precise statement

For i.i.d. returns with finite variance σ²: E[s²] = σ² exactly and E[σ̂²ₙ] = (n−1)/n · σ², with no distributional assumption. If the returns are moreover Gaussian, then (n−1)s²/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁, so Var(s²) = 2σ⁴/(n−1), and the sample standard deviation satisfies E[s] = c₄(n)·σ with c₄(n) = √(2/(n−1)) · Γ(n/2)/Γ((n−1)/2) < 1. Both estimators are consistent: bias and variance vanish as n → ∞.

The deeper cut — s underquotes σ even after Bessel

The desk does not quote variance; it quotes vol — the square root. And unbiasedness does not survive a square root. The root function is concave: pushing a scattered s² through it drags the average down, so E[s] < √E[s²] = σ strictly, whenever s² has any scatter at all. That is Jensen's inequality doing the shaving, not the divisor. For Gaussian returns the shortfall is an exact constant:

  • the vol-bias factor: always < 1, rising to 1 as n → ∞. c₄(20) ≈ 0.9869 — a 20-day vol quote runs about 1.3% below the truth, systematically.
  • the gamma function, the smooth extension of the factorial: Γ(k) = (k−1)! at integers. It appears because s² is χ²-distributed and E[√χ²] is a gamma-ratio integral.

Annualization inherits the shave multiplicatively: with true daily vol 1.2%, the honest annual number is 1.2·√252 ≈ 19.05%, but the average 20-day quote is c₄(20)×that ≈ 18.80% — roughly 25 bp of index vol, missing every month in the same direction. And because the culprit is Jensen, not the divisor, no choice of divisor under the root repairs it; only rescaling by c₄(n) (a normality-specific constant) does.

Accuracy is MSE — the divisor bake-off

Bias is only half of accuracy. The full score is mean squared error, MSE = variance + bias², and for Gaussian returns every divisor k applied to the same sum of squares SS = Σ(rᵢ−r̄)² has a closed form:

  • the shared numerator, distributed as σ²·χ²ₙ₋₁ under normality. All three candidate estimators are the same SS with different price tags.
  • the variance term (in units of σ⁴/k²). Bigger divisors shrink it — shrinking toward zero always cuts scatter.
  • the bias² term: zero exactly at k = n−1. Minimising the sum, not this term alone, gives k = n+1 — a little bias is worth buying.

The twist — unbiasedness is not the prize

The full MSE ordering is ÷(n+1) < ÷n < ÷(n−1) at every n: the "wrong" MLE beats the unbiased estimator, and a divisor nobody teaches beats them both. Trading a small, known bias for a larger cut in variance is a strictly better deal — this is the bias–variance trade-off in one line, the germ of every shrinkage estimator, and the exact tension Wing V reopens at the scale of neural networks. And the second shaving is stranger still: no divisor fixes s. The bias of s for σ comes from pushing scatter through a concave square root — Jensen's inequality — so it lives above the divisor entirely. Unbiasedness is not preserved by nonlinear functions; it is a property of a coordinate system, not of an estimator's quality.

Why this matters · volarixs

Every realized-vol input into the Cross-Matrix and every covariance entry feeding pca_factors is one of these two estimators evaluated on a short window — the choice of divisor is baked into every cell. On the 20-day windows the platform favours, ÷n versus ÷(n−1) is a systematic 5% shading of variance (the (n−1)/n = 0.95 factor), applied uniformly across the whole matrix.

Sharper still: at n = 20 the sampling noise of the estimate is not small relative to what's being traded. The relative standard deviation of s² is √(2/(n−1)) ≈ 32% of the variance level — roughly 16% on the vol quote itself — which is the same order as the vol moves a regime shift produces. A 20-day realized-vol series is an estimator's sampling distribution unrolled in time; treating its wiggles as pure market signal double-counts the noise you just watched in the histogram.
