Estimators, bias, and the n−1 story

Why dividing by n understates the spread — and why "unbiased" still isn't "best".

Level US Advanced undergraduate FR Licence L3 – Master M1 · ENSAE 1A

Same world as before: draws from a population with true variance σ² = 144 (σ = 12), but now both μ and σ² are unknown. To estimate the spread you sum squared deviations — from x̄, because μ isn't available. That single substitution is the whole story: x̄ is fit to your data, so it sits closer to the points than the true μ does, and the deviations come out systematically too small.

The two estimators
  • the sample mean we subtract. Being chosen to fit the sample, it minimises Σ(xᵢ−c)² over c — so deviations from x̄ are the smallest they can be, smaller than from the true μ.
  • the sum of squares (SS) — total squared spread around x̄. Both estimators share this numerator; they differ only in the divisor.
  • the MLE (divide by n). This is what maximum likelihood hands you for a normal — and it is biased low.
  • the Bessel-corrected estimator (divide by n−1). Exactly unbiased for the variance of any distribution with finite variance.
  • degrees of freedom: n data points minus the 1 constraint spent computing x̄. The correction is nothing more than "give back the freedom you used".

At small n the biased MLE's mean sits visibly left of the true line. Drag n up and the two distributions slide together — bias vanishes as n → ∞.

The precise statement

For i.i.d. data with finite variance σ², E[S²₍ₙ₋₁₎] = σ² exactly — unbiasedness needs no normality assumption. The MLE satisfies E[S²₍ₙ₎] = (n−1)/n · σ², biased downward. Both converge to σ² as n → ∞ (consistency); normality is required only for the estimator's exact sampling distribution, not for these expectations.

The twist — unbiased is not "best"

Accuracy is mean squared error, MSE = variance + bias². Minimising MSE over divisors S = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²/k gives, for normal data, k = n+1 — not n−1. Trading a little bias for enough variance reduction wins. The full ordering is MSE(÷(n+1)) < MSE(÷n) < MSE(÷(n−1)): the unbiased estimator has the worst MSE of the three, and the "wrong" MLE beats it. This is the bias–variance trade-off in one line, and the reason shrinkage estimators exist. Watch it below (clearest at small n):

Why this matters · volarixs

Variance bias isn't academic for you — it's the vol you annualise and, more sharply, the covariance matrices under your factor and regime models. On a short window (exactly when regimes shift and you want short windows), the ÷n estimator understates realised vol; the correction bites most when data is scarce.

One dimension up it gets worse: the sample covariance matrix is unbiased entry-by-entry but a poor estimate of the matrix once the number of assets approaches the number of observations — its eigenvalues fan out and the inverse (what any Markowitz or factor step needs) explodes. The fix is precisely the twist above: deliberately bias toward a simpler target to cut variance — Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage. Same bias–variance trade you just watched, now on Σ̂.
