Lesson 20 · Wing IV — inside the transformer

Embeddings as directions & superposition

d dimensions, m ≫ d things to represent — and it works, because almost nothing is happening at once.

Level US Graduate · ML FR M2 · ENSAE 3A

A layer of a network carries a vector of some fixed width d. The things it might want to track — is this a legal citation, is the speaker angry, is the vol regime stressed — number in the tens of thousands. The naive encoding gives each thing its own coordinate and runs out of room at d. The trick the network actually uses: give each thing its own direction, let the directions be almost perpendicular instead of exactly perpendicular, and lean on the fact that at any moment almost every thing is off. Non-perpendicular directions bleed into each other — but a bleed only costs you when two of them are on together, and under sparsity that is rare. That trade is superposition. Lesson 10 already paid for it: near-orthogonal directions are exponentially cheap in d. This lesson spends the money, in a toy model small enough to train live and watch.

1 · The words, before the machinery

Nothing below is a theorem yet. These are the nine words the rest of the lesson is written in, in plain English first.

  • featureone thing the network tracks, with a strength: off, or on at some intensity. "Mentions a French place-name" (0 or 1), "how bullish the sentence is" (a magnitude). We will index features by i and call the strength xi ≥ 0.
  • directiona fixed vector in the layer's space assigned to a feature. "Feature i is present at strength xi" is written into the layer by adding xi times that vector. Nothing else about the vector matters — its meaning is entirely which feature it is bookkeeping for.
  • linear representationthe hypothesis that features get directions and that several features present at once are stored by addition. Two claims, both empirical: one direction per feature, and composition = sum. Everything in this lesson assumes it; the twist says what happens if it is only approximately true.
  • residual streamthe d-dimensional vector every transformer block reads from and adds to — the space these directions live in (Lesson 19). d = 4096 in a mid-size model; the vocabulary alone is ~10⁵ items, before any abstract feature.
  • sparseat any moment almost all features are off. We write S for the probability a given feature is active, so sparsity is 1 − S. (Elhage et al. name the sparsity itself S; the slider below is labelled with the activity rate to keep "left = dense" honest.) Sparsity is a statement about bits: the on/off pattern of m independent features is m Bernoulli(S) draws, so saying which ones are live costs m·H₂(S) bits, where H₂(S) = −S log₂S − (1−S)log₂(1−S) is Lesson 15's entropy H(p) specialised to two outcomes and read in bits rather than nats — for m = 5 that is 5 bits at S = 0.5 but only 0.71 bits at S = 0.02. Almost no information in the pattern is exactly what makes the overbooking affordable.
  • interferencethe dot product between two feature directions — the crosstalk. Zero means reading one tells you nothing about the other. Non-zero means the read-out of feature i picks up a smear of feature j whenever j is on.
  • superpositionstoring m > d features on m non-orthogonal directions in d dimensions, paying interference and relying on sparsity to make collisions rare. The whole lesson in one line. Not compression of the features into fewer features — overbooking of the space.
  • polysemantica single coordinate (neuron) that responds to several unrelated features. If directions are not axis-aligned, every axis is a mixture. This is a prediction of superposition, not a symptom of sloppy training.
  • overcomplete dictionarya set of more than d directions in ℝᵈ, used as if it were a basis. It cannot be a basis — it is linearly dependent by counting. It can still be an almost-basis for vectors that only ever use a few entries at a time, which is what sparse dictionary learning exploits.
2 · The budget being spent (Lesson 10)

ℝᵈ holds exactly d mutually perpendicular directions — a hard ceiling, and the reason one-feature-per-neuron caps a model at d features. Lesson 10 showed that relaxing perpendicular to almost perpendicular blows that ceiling open. Two random unit vectors have cosine spread 1/√d about zero, so exceeding a tolerance ε is exponentially unlikely, and a random collection of that size stays pairwise within tolerance:

  • the interference tolerance — how much crosstalk between two directions you are willing to carry. It appears squared, so tolerating twice the bleed buys four times the exponent.
  • the chance a random pair violates the tolerance. Lesson 10's Gaussian cosine law, integrated in its tails; the 2 is the two signs of the cosine.
  • how many directions can coexist at tolerance ε — exponential in d. Read the exponent, not the constant. What is solid is that the count grows like e^{c ε²d} for a positive c: Lesson 10 quotes c = ½ from the pair-violation probability, while a strict union bound over all N(N−1)/2 pairs — requiring N²e^{−dε²/2} ≲ 1 — only licenses c = ¼. The constant depends on the construction and on whether you demand every pair in tolerance; the exponential in ε²d does not. This is why capacity figures here are order-of-magnitude readings.
  • a real residual stream. At ε = 0.1 the count is e^{20.48} ≈ 8×10⁸ — Lesson 10's figure. Hundreds of millions of usable slots in a space with 4,096 exact ones. Superposition is the mechanism that draws on that account.
3 · The toy model (Elhage et al., 2022)

The cleanest way to see a network choose superposition is to build the smallest problem in which it has to. m features go in, a d-dimensional bottleneck with d < m sits in the middle, and the same weights are used to read back out. Features are non-negative and sparse; the reconstruction is rectified so the model can threshold small crosstalk away; and features are unequally important, so the model has a reason to prefer some over others.

  • the true feature vector — m independent switches, each on with probability S and then uniform in [0,1]. Non-negative on purpose: real features are "present by this much", not signed. Independence is a simplification the twist returns to.
  • the encoder; its i-th column Wi ∈ ℝᵈ is feature i's direction. The entire object of study. A feature is "represented" exactly when ‖Wi‖ is not ≈ 0.
  • the bottleneck — the residual stream in miniature. h = Σi xiWi: the linear-representation hypothesis, imposed by construction rather than discovered.
  • the read-out, tied to the encoder (same W) plus a learned bias. Weight-tying is what makes the geometry legible: one direction per feature, used to write and to read.
  • the rectifier. Not decoration: with b < 0 it lets the model discard crosstalk that lands below threshold, which is precisely what makes negative interference cheap.

Training minimizes importance-weighted squared reconstruction error over the feature draw:

  • feature i's importance — how much its error costs. Geometrically decaying, I = (1, 0.8, 0.64, 0.512, 0.4096) for m = 5. Without a ranking the dense limit would have no reason to prefer one feature over another.
  • expectation over fresh feature draws. Estimated in the panel by a seeded mini-batch of 512 per step — this is Lesson 9's SGD, with Adam doing the rescaling.
  • plain squared error. Note what the model may do: give up on feature i entirely, set Wi = 0, and let bi predict the unconditional mean 𝔼[xi] = S/2. That costs exactly Ii·Var(xi) = Ii(S/3 − S²/4) — the price of a discarded feature, and a number we will check against the panel.

Everything interesting is in one matrix. Substitute h = Wx into the read-out and the model's own view of feature i falls out:

  • the interference matrix — the Gram matrix of the feature directions, the XᵀX object of Lessons 6–7 wearing new clothes. Rank ≤ d, so with m > d it can never be the identity: some off-diagonal must be non-zero. That impossibility is the lesson.
  • the diagonal: how strongly feature i is represented. ≈ 0 means the model has dropped it; ≈ 1 means it is carried at roughly unit gain.
  • the off-diagonal: how much j leaks into i's read-out. Its cost is paid only on draws where xj ≠ 0 and feature i is being read — probability ~S² per pair. Sparsity is not a nice-to-have, it is the discount rate on interference.
  • a negative bias as a noise gate. Positive leakage below |bi| is rectified to zero and costs nothing, which is why the trained biases go negative exactly when the model starts overbooking.

5 · The dense limit is PCA — and that part is a theorem

Push S to 1 and the model stops overbooking: it keeps exactly d = 2 features and throws the rest away. That is not an accident of the optimizer, it is Lesson 8. Let M = WᵀW, so rank M ≤ d. Suppose the ReLU is inactive on the relevant range and let b absorb the means, so the model is the linear map x̂ = Mx applied to centred features with Var(xi) = σ². Then the objective collapses onto a Frobenius norm:

  • the importance weights, folded into the norm. Row i of the residual is scaled by √Ii, so ΣiIi‖row i‖² is literally a Frobenius norm of a reweighted matrix.
  • the error operator. Minimizing ‖diag(√I) − diag(√I)M‖_F over rank-≤d second terms is Eckart–Young: approximate the diagonal matrix diag(√I) by a rank-d matrix.
  • the variance of a live feature at S = 1, Var(Unif[0,1]) = 1/12 ≈ 0.0833. A dropped feature therefore costs exactly Ii/12, and the sum over the m − d dropped ones is the loss floor the panel converges to.
  • the minimizer: 1 on the d largest importances, 0 elsewhere. Singular values of a diagonal matrix are its entries, so the best rank-d truncation keeps the d biggest — the d most important features, on orthogonal directions. PCA, by another road.

The panel confirms it numerically. At S = 1 after 400 steps it keeps features 1 and 2 — the two most important — at 90.3° apart, with the other three collapsed, and their biases sitting near 0.5 = 𝔼[xi]. The held-out loss reads 0.1297 against the predicted floor (I₃ + I₄ + I₅)/12 = 1.5616/12 = 0.1301. Now drop S: at S = 0.02 all five features survive, with every adjacent angular gap between 69.4° and 75.8° — inside 4° of the 360°/5 = 72° of a regular pentagon, in a two-dimensional space (it is that narrow spread that is the evidence: the mean gap is 72° for any five directions, because the gaps close the circle) — with mean |off-diagonal| 0.744 and every bias negative (mean −0.260). Five features, two dimensions, loss 0.0019.

The precise statement

Three claims, of three different kinds. (i) Hypothesis. The linear representation hypothesis asserts that a network's features correspond to directions vi in activation space and that co-occurring features compose additively, h ≈ Σi xivi. This is an empirical hypothesis about trained networks, not a theorem, and it is imposed by construction in the toy model above. (ii) Proved. For fixed ε ∈ (0,1) the maximum number Nε(d) of unit vectors in ℝᵈ with pairwise |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ε grows exponentially in d — specifically like e^{cε²d} for some c > 0, Lesson 10's random construction quoting c = ½ and a strict union bound over pairs licensing c = ¼; the exponent, not the constant, is the content. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma gives the dual form — m points embed into k = O(ε⁻²log m) dimensions with all pairwise distances preserved to 1 ± ε. Exact orthogonality is the only thing that costs d; ε-orthogonality is exponentially cheap. Sharp constants are open in general, so treat any capacity figure as an order of magnitude. (iii) Proved, in the dense limit. With S = 1, the rectifier inactive and the bias absorbing means, the toy objective equals σ²‖diag(√I)(𝕀−WᵀW)‖²_F with rank(WᵀW) ≤ d; by Eckart–Young–Mirsky (Lesson 8) the minimizer sets WᵀW to the identity on the d largest Ii and zero elsewhere, giving loss σ²Σi>dIi. Dense superposition is never optimal. What is not proved is everything in between: the phase diagram — which sparsity level buys which polygon, and how many features a real network superposes — is empirical, measured here and in Elhage et al. (2022), not derived.

7 · Re-applied — a factor model is a superposition

Strip the neural vocabulary and this is a risk model. You have m exposures — names, tenors, strikes, countries — and you insist on describing them with d ≪ m factors. The factor loadings are the feature directions: exposure i is the column βi ∈ ℝᵈ, the day's factor returns are the bottleneck h, and your reconstruction of exposure i is ⟨βi, h⟩ — the same read-out, with the same Gram matrix B⊤B (B the d×m loading matrix) deciding what bleeds into what. Three consequences transfer verbatim.

First, at d factors you get exactly d mutually independent stories and no more; everything past that is a near-orthogonal direction with real, if small, overlap. Second, which exposures can safely share a direction is a question about co-activation, not about correlation of returns: two exposures that are never live in the same window — a Q1 expiry and a Q4 expiry, a name pre- and post-delisting — can share a factor direction at zero cost, because their interference term is multiplied by xixj = 0. Third, the bill for the overbooking arrives as spurious cross-sensitivity: when two drivers do fire together, the model attributes part of one's move to the other. In Lesson 7's Cross-Matrix that is the collinear-driver problem exactly — the same ill-conditioned Gram matrix, the same sign flips across subsamples. Superposition is not an exotic AI phenomenon; it is what every low-factor description of a high-dimensional book has always been doing, now with the failure mode named.

The twist — the neuron was never the unit

Look at the pentagon again. There are two coordinate axes and five directions, so at most two features could ever be axis-aligned and the other three are forced into blends — read off either coordinate of the bottleneck and you are reading several features at once: each neuron fires for unrelated things. The interpretability literature spent years treating that as evidence of messiness, something better training would clean up. The toy model says the opposite: under sparsity, polysemantic neurons are what the optimal code looks like, and an axis-aligned model would be leaving the exponential budget of Section 2 unspent. Which means "what does neuron 4,182 do?" is a malformed question — you were reading a coordinate of a basis the network never chose. The repair is to stop asking about neurons and go looking for the directions: fit an overcomplete dictionary of n ≫ d unit vectors with a sparsity penalty, so that each activation vector is explained by a handful of them. That is a sparse autoencoder — and note what it assumes: the sparsity Section 1 merely observed, its m·H₂(S) bits, promoted to a penalty in the objective — in practice an L1 term (or a hard top-k) on the codes, standing in for the count of live features. It is the machinery behind Lesson 41. Second barrel, and it needs saying: this is a toy. Five features, two dimensions, independent uniform activations, weight-tied linear encoder — a real model has correlated features, many layers, attention writing into the same stream, and no ground-truth feature list to check against. That real LLMs superpose in this specific geometric way is a hypothesis supported by sparse-autoencoder results, not a proven property of trained transformers, and the honest position is that the phase diagram above is a mechanism we know is available and optimal in the toy, not one we have measured in the wild.

Why this matters · volarixs

pca_factors is the superposition budget, set by hand. Choosing k factors for a several-hundred-name universe is choosing d, and everything beyond the k-th eigen-portfolio is not "noise you removed" — it is structure you have folded onto directions you already use. The read: don't ask only where the singular spectrum bends (Lesson 8), ask which exposures the truncation has just made share a direction, and whether those exposures are ever live simultaneously. Sharing is free between things that never fire together and expensive between things that fire in the same crisis.

The Cross-Matrix pays the interference bill directly. Its driver panel is deliberately overcomplete — dispersion at five horizons, their z-scores, several correlated country vols — so the Gram matrix is ill-conditioned and a cell's β̂ absorbs part of its neighbours' moves whenever those drivers fire together. That is not an estimation bug to fix with a better optimizer; it is the same ⟨Wi, Wj⟩xj term as above, and it is exactly why sign_stability — not the t-stat — is the gate: a cell whose loading is mostly interference keeps its sign only in subsamples where the colliding driver happened to be quiet.
