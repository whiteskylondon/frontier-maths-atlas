Conditional expectation as a projection

The best predictor of Y from X, and why that sentence is geometry.

Level US Advanced undergrad → graduate FR Master M1–M2 · ENSAE 2A–3A

Ask for the function of X that predicts Y with the smallest mean squared error. The answer is E[Y|X] — and the reason is a picture: think of random variables as vectors, MSE as squared distance, and "functions of X" as a flat subspace. The best predictor is then just the closest point in that subspace — the orthogonal projection of Y onto it. Everything below is that one idea, made literal.

The claim, and the geometry

Give the space of random variables an inner product and the words "length", "angle", "distance" all acquire meaning:

The defining property — the residual is orthogonal to every function of X (there's no predictable structure left to use):

  • the conditional expectation — itself a random variable, a function of X. The projection of Y onto the subspace of all functions of X.
  • the inner product. Uncorrelated (mean-zero) variables are orthogonal; MSE is squared distance under it.
  • the residual — the part of Y unpredictable from X. Orthogonal to the whole subspace, by construction.
  • any predictor built from X. Constants, lines, curves — the richer the family, the bigger the subspace.

The precise statement

E[Y|X] is the orthogonal projection of Y onto L²(σ(X)), the space of square-integrable functions of X. It is the unique minimiser of E[(Y−g(X))²]; its residual is orthogonal to every function of X; and Var(Y) = Var(E[Y|X]) + E[Var(Y|X)] is the Pythagorean identity for that projection.

The twist — OLS is a smaller projection, and loss chooses the target

Least-squares regression projects only onto span{1, X} — straight lines. It equals E[Y|X] only when the conditional mean truly is linear; otherwise OLS is the best linear approximation and can hide real structure (watch corr(resid, X²) stay large in Linear mode — the line removed the trend but not the curve). And the target itself depends on the loss: minimising squared error gives the conditional mean, but minimising absolute error E|Y−g(X)| gives the conditional median, and a tilted absolute loss gives conditional quantiles — the whole of quantile regression. "Best predictor" is only defined once you fix what you're paying for errors.

Why this matters · volarixs

This is the operation under your cross-sectional machinery, not an analogy. A factor model is a projection: regressing returns on factor exposures projects Y onto the factor subspace, and your alpha is the residual — orthogonal to the factors by construction. R² = Var(E[Y|X])/Var(Y) is exactly "how much does this signal explain", read straight off the Pythagorean split.

Neutralising a signal against sector or size is this same move: project onto the nuisance subspace and keep the orthogonal residual. And the twist is a standing hazard for you — a signal can look predictive under a linear fit while E[Y|X] is genuinely nonlinear (saturation, regime-dependence). That's your regime cross-matrix in this language: the shape of E[Y|X] changing across conditioning states is the thing a single linear projection cannot see.
