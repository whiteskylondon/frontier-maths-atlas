Lesson 16 · Wing III — probability & information

The information bottleneck

Keep only the bits of today that matter for tomorrow — and put a price on the ones you keep.

Level US Graduate FR M2 – PhD

Every representation is a compression with an agenda. You look at today's market — vol regime, trend, everything — and you write down a summary. The summary is cheaper than the raw state, and that is the point; the question is what you threw away. The information bottleneck makes the trade explicit by scoring any summary T of an input X on exactly two numbers: how much it remembers about X (its cost) and how much it retains about the thing you actually care about, Y (its value). Then it prices one against the other with a single knob β and asks for the best summary at that price. Lesson 15 built the measuring instruments — entropy, KL, and the mutual information I that is a KL in disguise. This lesson points them at one question with a real answer: how many distinct market regimes are worth remembering, if all you care about is whether tomorrow closes up?

The vocabulary, in plain words
  • encoderthe rule that turns a state x into a summary t — allowed to be random: a conditional distribution p(t|x), not a function. Randomness is what makes the problem smooth and solvable; a hard partition is the β → ∞ limit of a soft one.
  • mutual informationhow much knowing one variable cuts your uncertainty about another, in nats. Zero exactly when they are independent. Lesson 15 note: I(A;B) is the KL from the joint to the product of margins — a divergence, not a correlation, so it sees non-linear dependence too.
  • cost axisI(X;T) — the bits your summary spends remembering X. Also called the rate: the channel width you need to transmit T given X's statistics.
  • value axisI(T;Y) — the bits of Y your summary still predicts. The only thing you are paid for. Everything else T remembers is decoration.
  • βthe price of prediction in units of memory. Small β: hoard nothing, predict nothing. Large β: pay any storage cost for one more bit of foresight. Physically an inverse temperature — high β freezes the summary into hard clusters, low β melts it into mush.
  • ceilingthe hard fact that no summary of X can know more about Y than X itself does. Processing never creates information. Its formal name is the data-processing inequality; it is what makes the value axis saturate.
  • sufficient statistica summary that gives up nothing about Y (Lesson 3's sufficiency). Minimal sufficient: the cheapest such summary. The bottleneck's β → ∞ limit is exactly this object, approached from the soft side.
  • phase transitiona value of β at which the optimal summary suddenly splits one cluster into two. Not a gradual thing: below it the extra cluster is worthless, above it the old solution is no longer even a local optimum.
The two axes, and the market joint

Both axes are the same functional evaluated on different pairs. Written as a divergence, mutual information is the KL from the true joint to the world where the two variables were independent — Lesson 15's KL, applied to a joint:

  • the joint the encoder induces. p(x) is the market's own state frequency; p(t|x) is the only thing you get to choose in this whole lesson.
  • Lesson 15's divergence, here from the joint to the independent product. So I ≥ 0 by Gibbs, with equality iff X and T are independent — the trivial encoder.
  • the same number as an uncertainty reduction. Symmetric in its arguments: I(X;T) = I(T;X). The conditional entropy H(T|X) is an expectation over x — Lesson 4's tower property doing quiet work.

The value axis is the identical construction on (T, Y), and the ceiling it must respect is the same construction on (X, Y) — which, expanded through the tower property, is the average over states of how far each state's outlook on tomorrow sits from the unconditional one:

  • the cluster's predictive posture: what does tomorrow look like, given only the summary. This single number per cluster (Y is binary here) is the whole content of T. Everything below is about matching postures.
  • the uncertainty about tomorrow left after seeing today. By Lesson 4, H(Y|X) = 𝔼ₓ[H(Y|X = x)] — a conditional expectation, hence a projection.
  • the predictive information in the market state: the total budget of forecastable bits. Not large. That is not a defect of the method; it is the market.

The joint used throughout this lesson is built once, seeded, and never redrawn. X is today's state, discretized into 8 cells: vol regime (lo / hi) × trend bucket (strong up / up / flat / down). Y is tomorrow's direction (up / down). Each cell carries a frequency p(x) and a conditional up-probability p(y = up | x); the design is deliberately financial — in the hi-vol regime the trend signal is muted, so those four cells huddle near a coin flip, while the lo-vol cells spread out. Unconditionally the market is almost exactly a coin flip: p(up) = —, H(Y) = — nats. Conditioning on the eight-cell read buys back H(Y) − H(Y|X) = — nats — that is I(X;Y) = — nats, or — bits, and it is the entire budget any encoder can ever spend. The eight per-cell postures run from — to —, and H(X) = — nats is what it costs to remember which cell you are in exactly.

The Lagrangian

Now trade the axes off. Minimize cost, maximize value, and let one scalar decide the exchange rate:

  • a functional of the encoder, minimized over all valid conditional distributions p(t|x). Not over functions x ↦ t: the feasible set is the whole simplex per state, which is why calculus applies at all.
  • the price. Sweeping β traces a frontier of solutions, one per price — the information plane curve you are about to drag along.
  • prediction is free to ignore: the minimizer is the trivial encoder, T ⫫ X, both axes zero. And it stays the minimizer for a while — see the one-liner below.
The ceiling — data processing

T is built from X alone: once you know x, the summary t tells you nothing further about tomorrow. That is a Markov chain T — X — Y, and it caps the value axis twice over:

  • the DPI ceiling: no processing of today's state can predict tomorrow better than today's state does. Proof in one line: chain-rule I(X,T;Y) two ways. I(X;Y) + I(T;Y|X) = I(T;Y) + I(X;Y|T); the first conditional term is 0 by the Markov property, the second is ≥ 0.
  • the same argument with the roles of Y and T swapped: you cannot retain more about Y than you remember about X. This is the inequality that kills small β.

Hence the dead zone, in one line. For any encoder whatsoever, using only I(T;Y) ≤ I(X;T):

  • first inequality: replace I(T;Y) by the larger I(X;T), which only lowers 𝓛 — legal because β > 0. Second: (1−β) ≥ 0 and I(X;T) ≥ 0, so the product is non-negative.
  • attained by p(t|x) = p(t), which makes both informations exactly zero. A minimum that is achieved is the minimum. Nothing about the joint entered this argument — the dead zone is universal.

And the trivial encoder p(t|x) = p(t) achieves 𝓛 = 0 exactly. So for every β ≤ 1 the optimum is to remember nothing: prediction priced below one nat-per-nat cannot pay for its own storage. The bound is sufficient, not tight — in this joint the first split waits until β ≈ —, the critical price at which the trivial fixed point stops being stable.

The solver — three self-consistent equations

Setting the variation of 𝓛 to zero under the normalization constraint (a Lagrange multiplier per state) gives three coupled equations. They are not a formula for the answer; they are a fixed point you iterate, exactly like Blahut–Arimoto or an EM loop:

  • a Boltzmann weight whose energy is a KL divergence. Plain reading: assign state x to the cluster whose posture about tomorrow matches x's own posture, at inverse temperature β. The distortion measure is not given by hand — it falls out of the variation. That is the elegant part of the whole theory.
  • the per-state partition function, Σ_t p(t)exp(−βKL). Normalization only; it carries no modelling choice.
  • the cluster prior — a rich cluster is easier to join. This factor is what lets clusters die: p(t) → 0 removes t from the competition permanently.
  • the cluster's posture is the membership-weighted average of its members' postures — Lesson 4's conditional expectation, verbatim. p(x|t) by Bayes from p(t|x)p(x)/p(t). Iterating these three is a descent on 𝓛; it converges to a stationary point, not necessarily the global one, which is why the interactive anneals β upward with warm starts.

The precise statement

Let X, Y have a fixed joint distribution and let T range over encoders p(t|x) with the Markov constraint T — X — Y. For each β > 0, any stationary point of 𝓛 = I(X;T) − β I(T;Y) under per-state normalization satisfies the Tishby–Pereira–Bialek self-consistent equations p(t|x) = p(t)Z(x,β)⁻¹exp(−β DKL(p(y|x)‖p(y|t))), p(t) = Σxp(x)p(t|x), p(y|t) = Σxp(y|x)p(x|t); alternating these three is a convergent descent on 𝓛. The attainable region is bounded by I(T;Y) ≤ min{I(X;Y), I(X;T)}, and its upper boundary — the information curve I(T;Y) = F(I(X;T)) — is concave and non-decreasing, with slope 1/β at the solution for price β; concavity makes the frontier's returns diminishing, and slope 1/β ≥ 1 is impossible, recovering the dead zone β ≤ 1. As β → ∞ the encoder hardens into a deterministic partition of X that is a minimal sufficient statistic of X for Y (Lesson 3): it attains I(T;Y) = I(X;Y), the ceiling, at the least possible I(X;T) — a lossless summary of exactly the predictive content, and nothing else.

The twist — the deep-learning claim, and its contested status

In 2017 Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby plotted the layers of a training network on exactly the plane you just dragged along — one point per layer per epoch — and reported two phases: a short fitting phase where both I(X;T) and I(T;Y) rise, then a long compression phase where I(T;Y) plateaus while I(X;T) falls. The reading was intoxicating: SGD spends most of its time not learning but forgetting, deliberately squeezing the nuisance bits out of its representations, and that — not the fitting — is where generalization comes from. It made the bottleneck look like the objective deep learning had been secretly optimizing all along.

Saxe et al. (2018) took the claim apart. Compression is not universal: swap tanh for ReLU and the compression phase can vanish while generalization is unaffected; where compression does appear it tracks activation saturation, not any information-theoretic imperative; and generalization improves during the fitting phase too. The deeper objection is measurement. In a deterministic network T = f(X) with continuous X, I(X;T) is infinite — every distinct input maps to a distinct activation vector, so the representation remembers everything. Every reported "compression" curve is therefore a curve of some estimator: histogram binning, a noise assumption, or a variational bound, and the phases can be artifacts of the bin width. Tishby's group replied, the exchange continued, and the honest summary today is: the IB objective is beautiful, exactly solvable, and genuinely useful when you build a stochastic bottleneck on purpose (the variational IB, and the reverse-KL machinery of Lesson 28's ELBO) — but whether ordinary trained networks follow an information-plane trajectory remains contested and open. Hold the objective; do not hold the empirical claim.

Why this matters · volarixs

The regime HMM is a fitted bottleneck, whether or not it was built as one. Its filtered state vector compresses the entire price history into a handful of numbers, and it is kept or discarded on exactly one criterion: does it still predict what you need tomorrow. That is the cost axis and the value axis, in production. And the perennially awkward question — how many regimes? — is not a matter of taste: it is "where does the information curve stop paying". This lesson's frontier is concave, so each extra regime buys strictly less prediction per nat of memory than the one before; the honest stopping rule is the price β at which the next split's gain in predictive information no longer covers its cost in state complexity.

Two operational reads, conservatively stated. First, the compression axis is already visible daily: the entropy of the filtered regime vector (Lesson 15's confusion meter) is a live proxy for how much the summary is committing to — near zero it has collapsed to one regime, near its ceiling it is refusing to compress. Second, the bottleneck's merge rule is a warning about how regimes should be defined at all: it clusters by predictive posture, not by feature proximity. Two states with opposite trend readings but the same forward outlook belong in one regime, and a regime taxonomy built from feature similarity — vol buckets, trend buckets — will not agree with the one built from what the states forecast. When a sign_stability read differs sharply between two cells you have grouped together, that is the bottleneck telling you the grouping was drawn on the wrong axis.
