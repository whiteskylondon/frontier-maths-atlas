Lesson 22 · Wing V — training-dynamics mysteries

Double descent

Test error rises, spikes, and then falls again — past the size where the model already fits every training point exactly.

Level US Graduate FR Master 2 – PhD

Classical statistics teaches one shape. Add parameters and error falls, because the model can finally represent the truth; keep adding and error rises, because the model starts fitting noise. A U, with the good model at its bottom. Modern practice keeps walking past the bottom, past the right-hand arm, past the point where the model reproduces the training set exactly — and finds that test error, having spiked, falls again. Two descents, with a catastrophe between them. This lesson makes the catastrophe computable in a page of linear algebra: it is not a deep-learning mystery, it is the smallest singular value of a matrix going to zero, and the second descent is which exact fit you choose once there are infinitely many. Lesson 21 told you loss falls smoothly with size; this lesson is about the one place where that promise breaks, and about why it breaks there and nowhere else.

The words, before the machinery
  • n, pn = training examples, p = parameters (here, features). The whole lesson is about their ratio, not their sizes. We hold n = 40 fixed and sweep p from 1 to 200.
  • interpolationfitting the training data exactly: every residual zero, training error zero. Classically a symptom of disaster. Here it is the normal operating regime — every model with p > n in this lesson interpolates.
  • interpolation thresholdthe smallest p at which exact fitting first becomes possible. For a generic design matrix that is exactly p = n: n equations, n unknowns. Below it you cannot fit; above it you can fit in infinitely many ways.
  • min-norm fitamong all β that interpolate, the one with the smallest Euclidean length ‖β‖₂. It is Lesson 8's pseudoinverse solution X⁺y. Which interpolant you pick is the entire story of the second descent — they all have zero training error, so training error cannot choose for you.
  • s₁ … s_rthe singular values of the feature matrix (Lesson 8), sorted largest first. Written s here, not σ, because σ is reserved for the noise level in this lesson. s_min is the last non-negligible one; r is the rank.
  • κthe condition number s_max/s_min: how uneven the information is across directions. Lesson 5's κ(XᵀX), square-rooted — same object, one level down.
  • γthe aspect ratio p/n of the feature matrix. γ < 1 tall (more rows than columns), γ = 1 square, γ > 1 wide. The threshold is γ = 1.
  • random featuresa fixed dictionary of p nonlinear functions of the input drawn once at random, then fitted linearly. The standard laboratory animal for this phenomenon: it has the capacity behaviour of a wide network with none of the optimisation mess, because the fit is a closed-form linear solve.
  • ridge λthe penalty that adds λ‖β‖² to the squared error. λ = 0 is the un-regularized fit; λ > 0 shrinks. Lesson 5 introduced it as the repair for a tiny eigenvalue; here it is the switch that turns double descent off.
  • excess test errormean squared gap between the fitted function and the noiseless truth, on held-out inputs. Normalised so that predicting zero everywhere scores 1.00. The σ² noise floor is not added in, so the curve shows only the estimator's own damage.
  • Marchenko–Pasturthe limiting law for the eigenvalue histogram of a large random Gram matrix. Its only role here: it says where the spectrum's two edges sit as a function of γ, and the lower edge touches zero exactly at γ = 1.
The classical picture — and the divergence hiding in it

Fit p features to n points by ordinary least squares. Lesson 2's decomposition splits the error into a squared bias that falls with p (a richer model can represent more) and a variance that rises with p (more coefficients, each estimated from the same n points). For an isotropic Gaussian design the variance term is available in closed form, and so is its counterpart on the other side of the threshold:

  • the approximation error of the best p-feature model. Non-increasing in p — this is the first descent, and nothing else is.
  • the noise variance per training label: y = f*(x) + σ·(standard normal). The only thing being amplified. Set σ = 0 in the panel below and the whole spike goes with it.
  • the classical variance inflation, from Breiman & Freedman (1983). Read the denominator: it is degrees of freedom left over, and it hits zero as p → n. The classical formula already diverges at the threshold — the U's right arm is not a gentle rise, it is the left face of a cliff.
  • the same term mirrored past the threshold (Hastie, Montanari, Rosset & Tibshirani, 2022). Now it is p that grows and the variance that decays — the second descent, in one symbol. Both branches blow up at γ = 1 and calm down away from it.
  • the bias of the min-norm interpolant. Not the same as A(p), and it moves the other way: shortening β shrinks the fit toward zero, so B rises with p — in the isotropic model B ∝ 1 − n/p, climbing from nothing at the threshold toward the full signal power. The second descent is the variance term collapsing faster than the bias climbs, not both falling together. Set σ = 0 in the panel below and what is left is B alone: its far end sits well above its value just past the cliff.
The model you can actually compute

Belkin, Hsu, Ma & Mandal (2019) named the shape and, decisively, exhibited it in a model with no network in it at all — random-features regression. That is what the panels below run, because it makes every claim checkable in closed form.

Inputs x ∈ ℝ⁸ drawn standard normal; p features φj(x) = √2·cos(ωjᵀx + bj) with ωj, bj drawn once and then frozen, so growing p appends features rather than redrawing them. The target is a fixed combination of the same dictionary with coefficients decaying like 1/j, so the first few features do most of the work. Stack the features into the n×p matrix X and fit:

  • the feature matrix, n×p, entry (i,j) = φj(xi). The same design matrix as Lesson 5, only now the columns are random nonlinear functions instead of named factors.
  • the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse VΣ⁺Uᵀ from Lesson 8. When p > n there are infinitely many exact fits; X⁺y is the shortest of them, and that choice is the implicit regularization the second descent runs on.
  • the ridge parameter. λ = 0 recovers plain least squares below the threshold and min-norm interpolation above it. Every λ > 0 is a different estimator, and the shape of the curve belongs to the estimator, not to p.

Everything the panels show comes from one SVD per model size. Writing X = UΣVᵀ, the fitted coefficients and their expected length are exact:

  • the filter factor on direction i. At λ = 0 it is 1/si — unbounded. At λ > 0 it never exceeds 1/(2√λ), whatever si does. That single inequality is the whole ridge story.
  • how much of the labels points along the i-th left singular direction. y = f + ε is the training-label vector, f its noiseless part — the n numbers f*(x₁) … f*(xn) stacked — and ε the σ-scaled noise. So uiᵀy splits into signal uiᵀf and noise; averaging over the noise draw gives the (uiᵀf)² + σ² above, exactly.
  • expectation over the noise draw only. The panels plot this closed form rather than a simulated average, so the curves carry no Monte-Carlo jitter: every wiggle you see is real structure in one seeded feature draw. Inputs and features are fixed; nothing is resampled when you move a slider.

Why it spikes — divide by the smallest number you have

Look again at the expected coefficient length. At λ = 0 every direction contributes [(uiᵀf)² + σ²]/si², and the last term of that sum has s_min² in the denominator. So the question "why does the fit explode at p = n?" reduces to "why does s_min collapse at p = n?" — and that is a fact about the shape of a random matrix, not about learning at all:

  • the Marchenko–Pastur edges for a matrix with i.i.d. entries. At γ = 1 the lower edge is exactly 0: a square random matrix has a direction it barely sees. Away from γ = 1, in either direction, the edge lifts off zero — that is why the disaster is local to the threshold.
  • the conditioning catastrophe at γ = 1. Lesson 5's tiny eigenvalue of XᵀX and Lesson 8's near-zero singular value, arrived at here by counting rather than by correlation: nothing about these features is collinear, there are simply exactly as many of them as there are equations.
  • the natural scale of a singular value when the entries have unit variance. Honest caveat, worth the ink: random features are not i.i.d. entries — the columns are correlated, so both measured edges sit far from the MP prediction. What survives untouched is the γ = 1 collapse, because it comes from squareness, not from the entry law.

The precise statement

(i) Minimum-norm interpolation. If rank(X) = n ≤ p, then {β : Xβ = y} is a non-empty affine subspace and X⁺y is its unique element of smallest Euclidean norm; equivalently X⁺y = limλ→0⁺(XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy. Every element of that set has zero training error, so the training loss does not distinguish them: the estimator does.

(ii) Conditioning at the threshold. For X with i.i.d. mean-zero, unit-variance entries and γ = p/n fixed, the spectral measure of n⁻¹XᵀX converges to the Marchenko–Pastur law on [(1−√γ)², (1+√γ)²] — plus, when γ > 1, an atom of mass 1 − 1/γ at zero, contributed by the p − n directions the data never sees; hence s_min/√n → |1−√γ| for the smallest non-zero singular value and κ → (1+√γ)/|1−√γ|, which diverges at γ = 1. For a square random matrix with i.i.d. sub-Gaussian entries the smallest singular value is of order n^(−1/2) times the typical scale (Rudelson–Vershynin), so κ ≍ n across entry distributions and not merely for Gaussians. Since E‖X⁺y‖² = Σᵢ[(uᵢᵀf)² + σ²]/sᵢ², the squared coefficient norm and the variance term diverge with it, at rate σ²/s_min².

(iii) It is a property of the estimator. Double descent is not a statement that parameters help. Nakkiran, Venkat, Kakade and Ma (2020) prove that for the well-specified isotropic linear model with the ridge penalty tuned optimally at each p, the expected test risk is monotone non-increasing in p — and in n. The spike, and therefore the second descent that undoes it, is what un-regularized minimum-norm interpolation looks like; optimally-regularized fitting has neither.

The twist — the lesson is not "bigger is better", it is "don't camp on the cliff"

The popular reading of double descent is that over-parameterization is free. The curve says something narrower and more useful: the interpolation threshold is a conditioning cliff, and you should not build there. Everything to the right of it is a recovery from a wound you inflicted by choosing γ = 1. In this lesson's model, at the noise level currently on the slider and with the ridge switched off, the best under-parameterized size scores — while the best over-parameterized size scores —: the second descent recovers, and it still does not get back below what a properly-sized classical model already had. Search the ridge as well and the best error anywhere on the (p, λ) grid is — — reached without ever fitting the training set exactly. Double descent is the shadow cast by refusing to regularize.

Second barrel, and it is the one that settles the question. The same shape appears where there are no parameters to add. Epoch-wise: hold the architecture fixed, train longer, and test error can rise, spike and fall again — Nakkiran et al. (2019) document this across ResNets and Transformers. Sample-wise: hold the model fixed and add data, and there is a band where more data makes generalization strictly worse. That happens in this lesson's model too — pin p at 60 and grow n through 60 and the error climbs by orders of magnitude before collapsing again — because the cliff is at γ = p/n = 1, and you can drive γ to 1 by growing the denominator just as easily as the numerator. A phenomenon that fires when you add data is not a phenomenon about capacity.

Re-applied — the factor zoo is a p/n problem

A risk model has p candidate factors and n days. That is this lesson with the labels changed, and desks reach γ ≈ 1 without trying: Cochrane's "factor zoo" is now several hundred published candidates, and a rolling one-year estimation window is 252 observations. Run 250 factors on 252 days and you are standing on the threshold — the feature matrix is effectively square, s_min collapses, and the fitted betas explode. Lesson 5 got you into that state through correlation between a handful of drivers; the zoo gets you there through sheer count, with no two factors especially alike.

The symptoms are the ones Lesson 5 catalogued — enormous coefficients, signs that flip on refit, hedge ratios that are individually meaningless while the fitted P&L looks fine — and the fixes are the ones Lesson 8 supplied: ridge, or drop the near-zero singular directions with a truncated pseudoinverse. Note what the panels above just demonstrated about those fixes: they are not merely prudent, they are the exact operation that removes the spike. And note what nobody in finance should take from this lesson: there is no second descent to walk to. Estimating 5,000 factors on 252 days would put you far past the threshold with a min-norm interpolant of one year of returns, which is a machine for fitting one year of noise. The usable half of the curve is the left flank, and the instruction is to stay well clear of its right edge.

Why this matters · volarixs

The Cross-Matrix (Lesson 7) runs into the left flank cell by cell. Its driver panel already carries dispersion at five horizons plus their z-scores plus several country vols — p is not small — while a regime-conditional cell is fitted only on the dates carrying that label, so n is whatever the regime gave you. Rare regimes are exactly the cells where p/n creeps toward 1, and they are the cells whose β you most want to believe. That is a γ diagnostic, and it is cheap: κ of the cell's Gram matrix, per cell, before reading the coefficient.

You already have the downstream symptom and the repair. sign_stability is what a near-threshold cell looks like from outside — the coefficient is huge, the fit in-sample is fine, and the sign flips across subperiods, which is precisely the small-s_min direction sliding. bayesian_pooled_elasticity is ridge under another name (shrink toward a sector or index prior), and pca_factors is the truncated-SVD route from Lesson 8: fit on the leading directions and refuse to divide by the last ones. The honest boundary: nothing in the Cross-Matrix interpolates, so the second descent is not yours to use. What is yours is the first half of the curve and the conditioning read that tells you when you have wandered onto its right edge.
