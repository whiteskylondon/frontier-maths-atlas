Lesson 15 · Wing III — probability & information

Entropy, cross-entropy & KL

Your loss is the world's entropy plus a KL penalty — and only the KL part is your fault.

Level US Advanced undergrad → graduate FR M1–M2 · ENSAE 2A–3A

Every training loss you will ever stare at — a classifier's, an LLM's, a directional model's — is built from one primitive: the surprise −ln p of what actually happened. Average it under the truth and you get entropy, the world's irreducible uncertainty. Average the surprise of your prices under the world's draws and you get cross-entropy, the loss you actually pay. Their gap is the KL divergence — the part of the bill that is your fault. This lesson carries Wing III's two locked finance examples: the entropy of a regime-probability vector (how confused is my regime read, in bits?) and the log-loss of a directional forecast (the honest scoreboard for up/down calls). The punchline is one identity, H(p,q) = H(p) + KL(p‖q), and one incentive theorem: under log-loss, honesty is the unique optimum.

The vocabulary, in plain words
  • surprisethe number −ln p(x): how startled you are when x happens, given you priced it at p(x). An event priced at 1 carries no surprise; a 1-in-a-million event carries a lot. The log is forced on you: it is the only choice that makes surprises of independent events add.
  • entropy H(p)the expected surprise when the prices are right — the uncertainty of the source itself. Zero when one outcome is certain, maximal when all are equally live. The floor: no forecaster, however good, averages below it.
  • cross-entropy H(p,q)the expected surprise when reality draws from p but you priced q. This is what "the loss" measures in almost every model you train. Always ≥ H(p); equal exactly when your prices are the truth.
  • KL divergencethe gap H(p,q) − H(p): the surcharge for pricing q when the truth is p. Non-negative, zero only if q = p. Not a distance — it is asymmetric and fails the triangle inequality, and the direction you pick has semantics.
  • nats vs bitsthe same quantity in different log bases: natural log gives nats, log₂ gives bits; divide nats by ln 2 ≈ 0.693 to convert. A fair coin is 1 bit = 0.693 nats per flip; three equally live regimes are log₂3 ≈ 1.585 bits.
  • proper scoring rulea grading scheme for probability forecasts under which your expected score is best when you report what you actually believe. Strictly proper: best uniquely at your true belief. Log-loss is strictly proper — the theorem that makes it the honest scoreboard.
Surprise, and its average

Rare events carry more information than common ones — a limit-down day tells you more than an ordinary Tuesday. The clean way to quantify that is −ln p(x): decreasing in p, zero at certainty, additive across independent events (probabilities multiply, logs add). Entropy is nothing more than this surprise averaged under the distribution's own prices:

  • the surprise of outcome x under prices p. p = 1 → 0 surprise; p → 0 → ∞. Hold that infinity: it returns with teeth below.
  • entropy — expected surprise, the source's own uncertainty. A property of the world, not of any model. Convention 0·ln 0 = 0: impossible outcomes contribute nothing.
  • the bounds on K outcomes. 0 at certainty; the ceiling ln K nats = log₂K bits at uniform — a consequence of Jensen's inequality, Lesson 14's convexity cashed in.
  • the same number in bits. For a 3-regime vector the ceiling is log₂3 ≈ 1.585 bits — the gauge in the interactive below tops out exactly there.

Cross-entropy, and the master identity

Now let the world draw from p while you priced q. Your average surprise is the cross-entropy; the excess over the world's own entropy is the KL divergence. The identity between them is one line of algebra — add and subtract ln pᵢ inside the sum:

  • cross-entropy — surprise of your prices q, averaged under the truth p. The expectation is under p: reality deals the cards, you priced them. Note H(p,p) = H(p).
  • KL divergence from q to p — the avoidable part of the loss. Everything a better model could recover; H(p) is what no model can.
  • one term of the penalty: positive where you underpriced (qᵢ smaller than pᵢ), negative where you overpriced. The positives always win on average — that is Gibbs' inequality, next.

Why can the penalty never be negative? Because −ln is strictly convex, and Jensen's inequality (Lesson 14: a convex function of an average never exceeds the average of the function) applies in one line:

  • equals Σ qᵢ over the outcomes p can produce — at most 1. So −ln of it is ≥ 0. Nothing but convexity and "probabilities sum to one".
  • equality forces q/p constant (strict convexity) and Σqᵢ = 1 — i.e. q = p exactly. The penalty vanishes only for the truth. Read twice, this same line is also the honesty theorem below.

The precise statement

Let p, q be probability vectors on a finite alphabet, with the convention 0·ln 0 = 0. (i) Chain identity: H(p,q) = H(p) + DKL(p‖q). (ii) Gibbs' inequality: DKL(p‖q) = 𝔼p[−ln(q/p)] ≥ −ln 𝔼p[q/p] = −ln Σi: pᵢ>0 qᵢ ≥ 0, by Jensen applied to the strictly convex t ↦ −ln t; equality holds iff q = p. (iii) Strict propriety of log-loss: for fixed p, the expected loss q ↦ 𝔼p[−ln q(X)] = H(p) + DKL(p‖q) is uniquely minimized at q = p, where it equals H(p). Statement (iii) is statement (ii) read as an incentive: the penalty is non-negative and vanishes only for the truth, so truthful reporting is the unique optimum. DKL is not a metric: it is asymmetric and violates the triangle inequality.

The loss is a negative log-likelihood

Lesson 3 chose parameters by maximizing Σᵢ ln f(xᵢ; θ). Modern ML minimizes an average of −ln qθ(xᵢ). These are the same act — and the bridge is two lines: group the sum by outcome value, and the empirical frequencies p̂ₙ appear on their own:

  • the empirical distribution: the fraction of the sample equal to x. The grouping step: n copies of −ln q(x), one per observation of x, average to p̂ₙ(x)·(−ln q(x)).
  • the training loss is a cross-entropy — between the data's empirical distribution and your model. Minimizing it ≡ maximizing likelihood (Lesson 3) ≡ minimizing the forward KL(p̂ₙ‖qθ), since the H(p̂ₙ) floor does not involve θ.

Specialize to the simplest forecast there is: a constant probability p̂ that tomorrow's session closes up. If a fraction f of the n days realized up, the average log-loss and its minimizer have closed forms:

  • the realized up-frequency of the sample — the empirical distribution of a binary outcome in one number. The strictly convex L has its unique stationary point at p̂ = f: report the frequency, nothing else.
  • the binary entropy −f ln f − (1−f) ln(1−f): the floor value of the loss. Everything above it is DKL(Bern(f)‖Bern(p̂)) — your fault, not the market's.

The twist — you are watching every LLM train

The honest forecaster above is next-token prediction in miniature: a language model is a directional forecaster whose "up/down" is a ~50,000-outcome softmax, trained on exactly this loss — the average −ln q(next token). So every LLM training curve you have ever seen is the master identity drawn against compute: H(data) + KL(data‖model), a floor that no architecture, optimizer, or budget can breach — the entropy of language itself — plus a shrinking KL, the only part gradient descent can touch (Lesson 21's scaling laws are curves of this KL falling with scale). Second barrel: KL's asymmetry is a design lever, not a defect. Minimizing the forward KL(data‖model) — what MLE and the pretraining loss do — is mass-covering: the model must keep weight on every regime the data visits, because pricing a possible one at zero costs ∞ (you built that infinity with the crisis slider). Minimizing the reverse KL(model‖target) is mode-seeking: commit to one regime, ignore the rest, pay nothing for what you never sample. Wings VI–VII run on this choice — the ELBO of Lesson 28 is a reverse-KL objective, and RLHF's KL leash (Lessons 33 & 35) uses it to keep a policy pinned near its reference. Which end of the asymmetry you hold decides whether your model hedges or commits.

Why this matters · volarixs

Your regime HMM emits a filtered probability vector every day — and this lesson says that vector carries its own confusion meter: its entropy in bits. Near 0 bits the model is effectively certain of the regime; near log₂3 ≈ 1.585 bits (for three states) it knows nothing. Gating regime-conditional signals on that number — full weight when entropy is low, de-risked when it spikes — is a model-honest throttle that needs no new estimation, just a read of the vector the HMM already produces.

Two more direct uses, conservatively stated. Any directional or sign forecast the platform emits (sign_stability-flavored calls included) should be scored by log-loss rather than hit-rate: it is the strictly proper scoreboard — hedging toward 0.5 and bravado toward 1 both cost, and the average is exactly the model's NLL. And the KL between today's filtered regime vector and yesterday's is a natural magnitude for a regime-shift alarm: small under drift, large under a lurch — with the caveat that KL is asymmetric, so the direction (today‖yesterday vs yesterday‖today) should be chosen deliberately and then held fixed.
