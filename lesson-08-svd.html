SVD & low-rank structure

Every matrix is a rotation, a scaling, and a rotation — and most of the scaling is negligible.

Level US Advanced undergrad → graduate FR Licence L3 – Master 1 · ENSAE 2A

The singular value decomposition is the master factorization of applied maths: any matrix, square or not, splits into an orthonormal rotation, a non-negative diagonal stretch, and another rotation. The stretch factors — the singular values — are sorted largest-first, and in real data they collapse fast. That collapse is why compression, PCA, dimensionality reduction, and low-rank fine-tuning all work: the object lives in far fewer dimensions than it appears to.

The decomposition

Left of the equals: a single matrix as a product of three. Right of it: the same matrix as a sum of rank-1 layers, each layer an outer product weighted by a singular value. Truncating that sum is the whole game. The singular values connect straight back to Lessons 6–7 — they are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix:

  • left singular vectors (orthonormal columns). Eigenvectors of AAᵀ — the observation-space directions (the XXᵀ side from Lesson 6).
  • singular values on the diagonal, σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0. The stretch along each direction; their decay is the effective dimensionality.
  • right singular vectors (orthonormal). Eigenvectors of AᵀX — the feature-space principal axes (the XᵀX side).
  • one rank-1 layer. A is the sum of these; the big-σ layers carry the signal, the small-σ layers the noise.

The reason it matters — Eckart–Young

Keeping the top k layers isn't just a low-rank approximation — it's provably the best one, in both Frobenius and spectral norm. This is the exact fact from your placement quiz:

The approximation error is just the tail of singular values you dropped. Fast decay ⇒ tiny tail ⇒ a handful of layers reconstructs almost everything — which is what you watched above.

The precise statement

Every real m×n matrix admits A = UΣVᵀ with U, V orthogonal and Σ diagonal with entries σ₁ ≥ … ≥ σ_r > 0 (r = rank). The σᵢ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of AᵀA (equivalently AAᵀ). The truncated sum Aₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ is the unique best rank-≤k approximation in every unitarily-invariant norm (Eckart–Young–Mirsky), with Frobenius error √(Σ_{i>k} σᵢ²). The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse is A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ.

The twist — SVD is the numerically honest regression

Lesson 5 solved least squares with (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, and Lesson 5's own twist warned that a tiny eigenvalue of XᵀX blows the inverse up. The SVD is the cure: the pseudoinverse A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ inverts each singular value separately, so you can see and truncate the near-zero directions instead of dividing by them — that's exactly what "truncated-SVD regression" and ridge do to collinear features. More broadly: exact low rank is rare, but approximate low rank is everywhere in ML — weight matrices, attention maps, embedding tables all have fast-decaying spectra, which is the entire premise behind LoRA (fine-tune a big weight W by a low-rank update ΔW = BAᵀ) and behind why models compress at all. The singular spectrum, not the nominal shape, is a matrix's true size.

Why this matters · volarixs

SVD is your pca_factors driver: run it on the centred returns panel and the right singular vectors are the principal components / eigen-portfolios, with σᵢ² the variance each carries — the same spectrum whose small tail was the collinear-driver instability in Lessons 5 and 7. "How many factors really drive the universe?" is literally "where does the singular spectrum bend."

Two operational uses: the numerical rank (count of non-negligible σ) is your honest count of independent drivers, a cleaner collinearity read than eyeballing correlations; and when a factor regression is ill-conditioned, fit it through the truncated pseudoinverse rather than the raw normal equations — you drop the noise directions instead of amplifying them.
