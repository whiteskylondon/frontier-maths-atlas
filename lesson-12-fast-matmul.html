Lesson 12 · Wing I — the linear-algebra substrate

Fast matrix multiplication as a frontier

The most-run operation in computing has an exponent nobody has pinned down — and an AI has been hunting for it.

LevelUS GraduateFR M2 – PhD

Rebuilding the Cross-Matrix of Lessons 7 and 11 is one big matrix multiplication: einsum('at,bt->ab') on a 500-asset × 2,500-day returns panel. Each of the 500² = 250,000 output cells is a dot product across 2,500 days, so the build costs about 500·500·2,500 ≈ 6.25×10⁸ multiply-adds. This lesson is about what "one big matmul" fundamentally costs. Done by the definition, cost grows as n³ in the matrix size. In 1969 Volker Strassen showed that a 2×2 product needs only 7 multiplications instead of 8 — and recursion turns that single saved multiplication into an exponent below 3. How low the exponent can go, a constant called ω, is a famous open problem; in 2022 an RL system, AlphaTensor, joined the search by playing algorithm discovery as a game.

The words, before the symbols
  • multiply-addone scalar multiplication feeding an accumulator — the unit we count. Hardware fuses them (FMA); theory counts multiplications alone because, as you'll see, only multiplications recurse.
  • exponent ωthe smallest power τ such that n×n matmul can be done in roughly n^τ operations. "Roughly" hides constant factors — exactly where theory and practice part company.
  • blocka sub-matrix treated as a single entry. An identity written for 2×2 numbers runs verbatim on 2×2 blocks — that is what makes one small trick global.
  • bilinearan algorithm whose every multiplication takes one (± sum of A-entries) times one (± sum of B-entries), with outputs assembled by additions. The model in which "number of multiplications" is a well-defined complexity measure.
  • tensora 3-indexed 0/1 array recording which product aᵢₖ·bₖⱼ feeds which output cᵢⱼ. Matrix multiplication as data instead of code — a fixed algebraic object.
  • rankthe fewest one-multiplication pieces that sum to that tensor. Lesson 8's rank with three indices instead of two — and it equals the multiplication count of the best bilinear recipe.

One honesty note before the fireworks: at Cross-Matrix size (n in the hundreds), tuned classical BLAS wins on constants, memory traffic, and error bounds. The exponent story is about asymptotics — and about what exhaustive discrete search can discover. Both matter; they answer different questions.

The trick — Strassen's seven products

The naive 2×2 block product uses 8 block multiplications, one per (row, column, term). Strassen's observation: seven cleverly chosen products of ± sums of blocks carry enough information to assemble all four outputs by additions alone. Nothing is approximated — it is an algebraic identity.

  • the four blocks of each input. Scalars at the recursion floor; matrices above it — where each Mᵢ becomes a recursive call.
  • the seven products — the only multiplications performed. Each is (± sum of A-blocks)·(± sum of B-blocks): exactly the bilinear form above.
  • the outputs, assembled by additions alone. The classic form spends 18 block additions (10 building the Mᵢ, 8 assembling C); at size n they cost O(n²) — invisible next to the multiplications' recursion.

From one saved multiplication to an exponent

Run the identity on n×n matrices by splitting into 2×2 blocks of size n/2 and recursing: seven half-size subproblems, plus the 18 block additions. That recurrence is solved by the master theorem — the standard cookbook for divide-and-conquer costs:

  • seven recursive half-size products. The branching factor is the whole game: recursing on 8 would give n³ right back.
  • the 18 block additions at this level. Master theorem: since log₂7 > 2, the leaves beat the per-level work — additions never touch the exponent.
  • 7 raised to the number of levels: 7^(log₂n) = n^(log₂7). One recursion level on a 4×4 already shows it: 7² = 49 multiplications where the definition uses 4³ = 64.

The race since 1969: ω ≤ log₂7 ≈ 2.8074 (Strassen) → 2.376 (Coppersmith–Winograd, 1990) → 2.3728639 (Le Gall, 2014) → ≈ 2.3716 (Duan–Wu–Zhou and Williams–Xu–Xu–Zhou refinements, 2023–24). Every step is an upper bound on ω; the only lower bound known is the trivial ω ≥ 2 — you must at least touch all n² entries of each input. Whether ω = 2 is one of the great open problems of computer science.

The real object — a tensor and its rank

Strip away the code and matrix multiplication is a fixed algebraic object: the tensor ⟨m,n,p⟩ from the vocabulary. A bilinear algorithm with r multiplications is exactly a way of writing that tensor as a sum of r rank-1 pieces — so "how fast can we multiply" is literally "what is the rank of this tensor": Lesson 8's question, one index higher.

  • the matmul tensor for an (m×n)·(n×p) product. An mnp-entry array of 0/1s — fixed, independent of any algorithm.
  • one rank-1 piece = one multiplication: which combination of A-entries, times which combination of B-entries, routed where. The three-index analogue of Lesson 8's σuvᵀ layer.
  • best exponent over all finite decompositions, at every block size. Any single finite rank bound instantly caps ω — the lever the whole subject turns on.

The precise statement

In the bilinear model, an (m×n)·(n×p) matrix product computable with r scalar multiplications exists if and only if rank⟨m,n,p⟩ ≤ r; recursing a rank-r decomposition of ⟨n,n,n⟩ yields an O(N^{log_n r}) algorithm, and ω = inf{τ : rank⟨n,n,n⟩ = O(n^τ)}. Strassen's construction shows rank⟨2,2,2⟩ ≤ 7, and Winograd and Hopcroft–Kerr proved 7 is optimal, so rank⟨2,2,2⟩ = 7 and ω ≤ log₂7 ≈ 2.8074. The current record is ω < 2.3717. Trivially ω ≥ 2, and whether ω = 2 is open.

Multiplication counts on a log-log scale, n up to 2¹⁴: power laws are straight lines and their slopes — 3, 2.8074, 2.3716 — are the exponents. An AI joins the hunt

The tensor view turns algorithm discovery into a discrete search: find r rank-1 pieces summing exactly to ⟨n,n,n⟩. No gradients — a candidate decomposition is either an identity or garbage. For ⟨2,2,2⟩ the answer is settled at 7. For 4×4, the record stood at recursed Strassen's 7² = 49 for 53 years, until AlphaTensor (Fawzi et al., Nature 2022) cast decomposition-finding as a single-player game for the AlphaZero engine (Lesson 34) and found a rank-47 decomposition valid in arithmetic mod 2 — and, in standard arithmetic, improvements such as 4×5 by 5×5 in 76 multiplications, down from 80.

  • arithmetic mod 2 — bits. Tensor rank can depend on the field: the 47 is proven over Z₂ and does not automatically transfer to the reals.
  • base 4 because recursing ⟨4,4,4⟩ divides n by 4 per level: T(n) = 47·T(n/4) + O(n²). In general, rank⟨k,k,k⟩ ≤ r gives ω ≤ log_k r.
  • the 4×5 by 5×5 record in standard arithmetic. Rectangular records matter too — recursive constructions can mix shapes.

The twist — one finite number moves an infinite asymptote

Block recursion is a lever: a single finite discovery — one rank number for one small tensor — lowers the exponent for all n at once. AlphaTensor's 47 is not "4% better than 49"; recursed, it moves the asymptote over Z₂ from n^2.8074 to n^2.7773, for every matrix size forever. That is why people hunt tiny tensors: the ⟨2,2,2⟩ record fell once, in 1969, and the search space is so brutally discrete — exact decompositions, astronomically many candidates, no gradient to follow — that the 4×4 record then stood 53 years until decomposition-finding was reframed as a single-player RL game. An AI system nudging frontier mathematics forward is the bridge to Lesson 42's AlphaProof. The engineering counterweight keeps it honest: each Strassen recursion level weakens the floating-point error bound (you lose classical matmul's entrywise stability guarantee), so speed bought by exponent is partly paid in precision — one more reason production BLAS mostly declines.

Why this matters · volarixs

The Cross-Matrix rebuild is this lesson's object: einsum('at,bt->ab') on the 500×2,500 returns panel is a (500×2,500)·(2,500×500) product — 500·500·2,500 = 6.25×10⁸ multiply-adds per rebuild — and pca_factors runs its eigendecomposition on top of that output. Knowing what a matmul fundamentally costs is knowing what your hottest loop costs.

The operational lesson is diagnosing when cost is exponent-bound versus memory-bound. At 500×2,500 it is memory-bound: tiled classical BLAS — the n³ algorithm with cache-aware blocking — beats Strassen there on constants, traffic, and error bounds, so intraday rebuilds should stay on it, and they do. The exponent starts to matter orders of magnitude past desk size; this lesson is what that frontier is, not a to-do for the stack.
