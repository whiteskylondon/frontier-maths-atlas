Fast matrix multiplication as a frontier

The most-run operation in computing has an exponent nobody has pinned down — and an AI has been hunting for it.

LevelUS GraduateFR Master M2 – PhD

Multiplying two n×n matrices the obvious way costs about n³ scalar multiplications. For decades that looked fundamental — until Strassen showed in 1969 that a 2×2 block product needs only 7 multiplications instead of 8, and recursion turns that into an exponent below 3. The true exponent ω — how fast matrix multiplication can be — is one of the great open problems, and in 2022 an RL system (AlphaTensor) joined the search by treating the question as a game.

Three regimes of cost

Strassen's saving compounds through the recursion — you recurse on 7 subproblems instead of 8, so the count is 7 per level rather than 8:

  • n³the naive count. Each of n² outputs is a length-n dot product — the definition, nothing clever.
  • ωthe exponent of matrix multiplication. The infimum of exponents achievable; best known ≈ 2.371, and whether ω = 2 is open.
  • Rtensor rank of the matmul map. The number of scalar multiplications in a bilinear algorithm — this is what bounds ω.

The trick itself — Strassen's 7 products

For 2×2 blocks, seven cleverly chosen products of sums of blocks suffice; the four output blocks are then just additions of those seven. No block is multiplied twice.

M₁=(A₁₁+A₂₂)(B₁₁+B₂₂) M₂=(A₂₁+A₂₂)B₁₁ M₃=A₁₁(B₁₂−B₂₂)

The precise statement

Bilinear matrix multiplication of n×n matrices is encoded by the tensor ⟨n,n,n⟩; the minimum number of scalar multiplications in any bilinear algorithm equals that tensor's rank R. A rank-R decomposition of ⟨n,n,n⟩ yields an O(N^{log_n R}) recursive algorithm, so ω = inf over such constructions. Strassen's rank-7 decomposition of ⟨2,2,2⟩ gives ω ≤ log₂7 ≈ 2.807; successive results (Coppersmith–Winograd and descendants) push the best known bound to ω ≈ 2.371. Whether ω = 2 — multiplication essentially as cheap as reading the input — is open.

The twist — a computation is a tensor rank, and an AI went looking

The deep reframing is that "how fast can you multiply matrices" is literally "what is the rank of the matmul tensor" — a question in algebraic geometry, the same rank idea as the SVD (Lesson 8) lifted to three indices. That turned the search into something a machine could play: AlphaTensor (2022) used the AlphaZero engine — the MCTS-plus-RL core you'll meet in Wing VII (Lesson 34) — to search the space of tensor decompositions as a single-player game, and found new, lower-rank schemes for certain sizes and fields (including a 4×4 improvement over Strassen-squared in mod-2 arithmetic). Two honest caveats keep this grounded. The exotic ω ≈ 2.37 algorithms are galactic: their hidden constants are so enormous they never beat Strassen or naive on any matrix that fits in the universe — they're existence proofs, not code. And even Strassen only overtakes naive past a crossover size and trades multiplications for extra additions and memory, so tuned libraries use it selectively. The frontier is real mathematics; the practical winner is still mostly n³ with good cache behaviour.

Why this matters · volarixs

Matrix multiplication is the hot loop under almost everything you compute — every XᵀX, every covariance and factor-model fit, every backtest that projects a big panel. In practice the tuned BLAS underneath NumPy already exploits these ideas (blocking, and Strassen-style kernels for large sizes), which is why the cost scales closer to n^2.8 than n³ at scale and why matrix shape, not just size, drives runtime.

The connective thread to the rest of the wing: rank governs cost everywhere — low SVD rank compresses a matrix (Lesson 8), low tensor rank compresses the act of multiplying (here). And AlphaTensor is a clean preview of your own thesis — RL discovering mathematics — that lands fully in the reinforcement-learning wing.
