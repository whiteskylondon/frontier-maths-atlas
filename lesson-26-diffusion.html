Lesson 26 · Wing VI — generative machinery

Diffusion as SDEs + score matching

Destroy the structure on purpose, in a process you can write down in closed form — then learn the one unknown you need to run it backwards.

Level US Graduate FR Master 2 – PhD

Take any data distribution and stir noise into it, gradually, until nothing is left but a standard Gaussian. That destruction is easy: it is a mean-reverting stochastic differential equation whose solution at every intermediate time is available in closed form, so you can jump to any noise level in one line without simulating anything. The remarkable part is that the destruction is reversible, and that reversing it needs exactly one object you do not already have — the score, the gradient of the log density in x. Learn the score, integrate the reverse process, and noise becomes samples. This lesson builds the four load-bearing pieces: the forward SDE and its closed-form marginal, the identity that makes "predict the noise" and "predict the score" the same task, the theorem that lets you replace an uncomputable objective with a computable one at zero cost, and the deterministic ODE that turns the whole apparatus into an exactly invertible flow.

The words, before the machinery
  • SDE, dWa stochastic differential equation is a rule dx = f(x,t)dt + g(t)dW: over a tiny interval dt, move by f·dt and add an independent random kick. f is the drift, g the diffusion coefficient, and dW the increment of a Wiener process (Brownian motion) — a draw from 𝒩(0, dt·I), independent of everything before it. The Fokker–Planck equation is the deterministic PDE governing how the density of the solution evolves, ∂tp = −∇·(fp) + ½g²Δp, with Δ the Laplacian and ∇· the divergence. Two facts we lean on: variances of independent increments add, so integrating √β dW from 0 to t accumulates variance ∫β and not (∫√β)²; and Fokker–Planck, used once, gives the probability-flow ODE.
  • Ornstein–Uhlenbeckthe mean-reverting SDE dx = −κ(x − θ̄)dt + σdW: pulled back toward a level θ̄ at speed κ while being shaken. A rates desk calls the same equation Vasicek. Everything below is the θ̄ = 0 case with a time-varying speed.
  • β(t), ᾱtβ is the noise schedule, a positive function on [0,1] setting both the pull-back speed (β/2) and the shaking (√β). ᾱt ("alpha-bar") = exp(−∫0tβ) is the fraction of the original signal's variance still present at time t. We use the standard linear schedule β(t) = 0.1 + (20 − 0.1)t — a design choice, not a law. On it ᾱt falls from 1 to 4.32×10⁻⁵.
  • kernel, marginalthe perturbation kernel p(xt|x0) is the law of the noised point given the clean one; the marginal pt(x) = ∫p(x|x0)p0(x0)dx0 integrates the clean point out. The kernel is Gaussian and known exactly. The marginal is easy to sample from and, for anything real, impossible to write down. That gap is the whole difficulty.
  • score, sθthe score is ∇xlog p(x): the direction at x in which the log density rises fastest, and how fast — a vector field, one arrow per point. sθ is a neural network trained to output it given (x, t). Careful with the name: classical statistics (Lesson 3) calls ∇θlog pθ the score. This is the other one, the gradient in x, sometimes called the Stein score. In the panels below nothing is trained — the toy's score is closed-form, so the demonstrations are exact.
  • εthe standard Gaussian draw, ε ∼ 𝒩(0,I), used to jump straight to noise level t. Writing xt as a deterministic function of (x0, t, ε) is exactly Lesson 14's reparameterization: the randomness is frozen, the parameters enter smoothly, and gradients (Lesson 13) flow.
  • denoiser𝔼[x0|xt], the conditional mean of the clean point given the noisy one — the minimum-mean-squared-error estimator. Lesson 4's conditional expectation wearing a different hat. Tweedie's formula below says it is the score in disguise.
  • the toyeverything is demonstrated on one fixed 2-D mixture of three Gaussians: weights wk = (0.45, 0.32, 0.23), means μk = (−1.55, −0.85), (1.45, −0.75), (0.10, 1.70), each isotropic with σ0 = 0.28. Chosen because a Gaussian mixture stays a Gaussian mixture under the kernel, so pt and its exact score are closed-form at every t. No network, no approximation, nothing to distrust.
The forward process, and why you never have to run it

The variance-preserving (VP) forward diffusion is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process pulled toward the origin, with the pull and the shaking tied together by one schedule. Its drift is linear and its diffusion depends only on t, so — unusually — it integrates in closed form, and the second line below is the only line of code diffusion training ever needs:

  • the drift: a pull toward the origin at speed β(t)/2. The ½ is chosen precisely so signal decay and noise injection balance — writing d for the dimension of x (d = 2 in the panels), if 𝔼‖x0‖²/d = 1 then 𝔼‖xt‖²/d = 1 for every t, since ᾱt + (1 − ᾱt) = 1. That balance is what "variance preserving" names.
  • the shaking: independent Gaussian kicks of variance β(t)dt per coordinate. The same β as the drift — one schedule controls both, which is what makes ᾱt a single sufficient summary of "how far along" you are.
  • the closed-form signal fraction. On our linear β, ∫0tβ = 0.1t + 9.95t², so ᾱt = exp(−0.1t − 9.95t²) with no numerical integration. ᾱ1 = e−10.05 = 4.32×10⁻⁵, so whatever p0 was, p1 differs from 𝒩(0,I) by under 1.6×10⁻³ in each mean coordinate and 4.1×10⁻⁵ in every covariance entry.
  • the frozen noise. This is why diffusion is cheap to train: to build a training pair at noise level t you draw one ε and add, rather than stepping an SDE. The forward process is never simulated.
The one identity that makes it trainable

Differentiate the log of that Gaussian kernel in xt. Two lines of algebra, and the result is the pivot of the entire field:

  • the score of a Gaussian: minus the residual over the variance. For 𝒩(m, vI), ∇log p(x) = −(x − m)/v. Here m = √ᾱtx0 and v = 1 − ᾱt. Nothing deeper is happening.
  • the same thing rewritten through the reparameterization. The residual is √(1−ᾱt)·ε by construction, so the kernel's score is the noise you added, negated and divided by √(1−ᾱt) — one power of the standard deviation, not two. A network trained to predict ε and one trained to predict the score are the same network up to that scalar, which is why DDPM's ε-prediction loss and the score-matching loss are one objective in different clothes.
Running time backwards

Anderson (1982) proved that a diffusion run in reverse is again a diffusion, with one extra term in the drift. Song et al. (2021) observed that the same marginals are also carried by a deterministic flow — with the score coefficient halved. Both facts, for our forward SDE:

  • a Wiener increment in reverse time; dt is a negative increment. Concretely, one Euler step from t down to t − Δ is x ← x + Δ[½βx + β∇log pt] + √(βΔ)·z with z ∼ 𝒩(0,I). That is literally the loop in the panel below.
  • the only unknown. Everything else — β, the ½, the √β — is your own schedule. Diffusion modelling is the problem of estimating one time-indexed vector field, and nothing else.
  • the halved score in the ODE. Where the ½ comes from: Fokker–Planck's diffusion term rewrites as ½g²Δp = ∇·(½g²p∇log p), so folding all of it into the drift gives velocity f − ½g²∇log p and turns the equation into a pure continuity equation — deterministic transport with the same pt. Using β instead of ½β is the classic error: a perfectly valid ODE with the wrong marginals.

The objective you can compute

To learn the score you would like to minimise the distance between your network and the true score of the marginal. You cannot: pt is an integral over the whole dataset and ∇log pt is unavailable. Vincent (2011) showed you may replace it, exactly, by the distance to the score of the conditional kernel — which the identity above says is just the noise you added:

  • a constant containing no θ. So the two objectives have identical gradients in θ, identical minimisers, and identical optimisation trajectories under SGD (Lesson 9). Only their values differ — which is why a diffusion training loss is not comparable across noise levels, and why reported loss numbers are close to meaningless.
  • the leftover: the spread of the conditional score across the different clean points x0 that could have produced this xt. It is the irreducible ambiguity of denoising, and it blows up like d/(1−ᾱt) as t → 0. The panel above prints an estimate of it live.
  • the network being fitted, taking the noisy point and the noise level. In practice one trains a network εθ predicting ε and sets sθ = −εθ/√(1−ᾱt); the two parameterisations differ only by a per-t weighting of the loss.

The precise statement

Let p0 have finite second moment, let β be positive and continuous on [0,1], ᾱt = exp(−∫0tβ), and let the perturbation kernel be p(x|x0) = 𝒩(√ᾱtx0, (1−ᾱt)I), so that for every t > 0 the marginal pt = ∫p(·|x0)dp0 is smooth and strictly positive. (i) Denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011). Since ∇xpt(x) = ∫∇xp(x|x0)dp0, dividing by pt(x) gives 𝔼[∇log p(xt|x0) | xt] = ∇log pt(xt). Expanding both squares, the cross terms of JDSM and JESM therefore agree for every θ, and at each fixed noise level JDSM(θ) = JESM(θ) + Ct with Ct = 𝔼‖∇log p(xt|x0)‖² − 𝔼‖∇log pt(xt)‖² = 𝔼[tr Cov(∇log p(xt|x0)|xt)] ≥ 0, independent of θ. Hence the two objectives share a gradient in θ and a set of minimisers, and the minimiser over all measurable s is s(x,t) = ∇log pt(x).

(ii) Time reversal and probability flow (Anderson, 1982; Song et al., 2021). If dx = fdt + gdW has marginals pt, the reversed process satisfies dx = [f − g²∇log pt]dt + gdW̄ with dW̄ a reverse-time Wiener increment and dt < 0. Separately, because ½g²Δp = ∇·(½g²p∇log p), the Fokker–Planck equation for the same pt is the continuity equation ∂tpt = −∇·(vtpt) with velocity vt = f − ½g²∇log pt; the deterministic ODE dx/dt = vt(x) therefore has exactly the marginals pt at every t, with the score coefficient half that of the reverse SDE. For f = −½βx, g = √β these are the two equations displayed above.

(iii) Tweedie's formula (Robbins, 1956; Tweedie). For the Gaussian kernel above, 𝔼[x0|xt] = (xt + (1−ᾱt)∇log pt(xt))/√ᾱt, exactly and for every t > 0 — no small-noise limit. The minimum-MSE denoiser and the score are the same object under an affine change of variables.

The denoiser is the score

Statement (iii) deserves its own line, because it is what a practitioner actually feels. Nobody sets out to estimate a log-density gradient; they train a network to clean up a noisy picture. Tweedie says those are the same job, and the relation inverts:

  • the denoiser, trained by plain mean-squared error against the clean point. Read left to right: a denoiser hands you the score. Read right to left: a score model hands you the optimal denoiser, which is why the same checkpoint does inpainting, super-resolution and unconditional sampling.
  • the conversion factor, and the reason the two views drift apart numerically. At small t the division by 1−ᾱt amplifies any denoiser error enormously, which is exactly the regime where the DSM offset Ct also blows up; at large t the two are on the same scale.

For the toy both sides are closed form. The marginal stays a mixture, with every centre shrunk by √ᾱt and every variance inflated:

  • the marginal variance per coordinate: shrunk data variance plus injected noise. Runs from σ0² = 0.0784 at t = 0 up to 0.99996 at t = 1. Its square root is the yardstick the panel below measures mode separation in.
  • the responsibility: the posterior probability that x came from component k, rk ∝ wk𝒩(x; √ᾱtμk, vtI). So the exact score is a responsibility-weighted average of three simple Gaussian scores — Bayes' rule, then a weighted mean. As t grows the rk flatten toward the prior weights wk and the field forgets which mode is which.

Re-applied — the schedule is a term structure

Nothing in the forward process is new to a rates desk. Substituting κ(t) = β(t)/2 and σ(t) = √β(t), it is Vasicek with a zero reversion level and a time-varying speed, and the quantities diffusion papers call the "signal rate" and the "noise rate" are the two halves of the standard OU solution:

  • the decay of the initial condition — how much of where you started still shows. The half-life solves ∫0tβ = 2 ln 2 = 1.3863; on β(t) = 0.1 + 19.9t that is 9.95t² + 0.1t − 1.3863 = 0, i.e. t = 0.36827. Over a third of the schedule is spent losing the first half of the signal.
  • the accumulated variance as a function of horizon — a term structure of variance, in the ordinary sense. Choosing a noise schedule is choosing that curve's shape; cosine and linear schedules differ only in how they front- or back-load it, exactly as two variance term structures differ.

The genuinely useful transfer is the generative one. If you want to sample scenarios from a fitted joint distribution of returns without committing to a parametric family — no multivariate Gaussian, no fitted copula, no assumed tail — a score model is a legitimate way to do it: it learns the density's gradient field from samples and generates new ones by integrating, and the panels above are a scaled-down version of exactly that. Two honest caveats before anyone builds it. First, this is a research technique, not desk practice: score models earn their keep in the many-samples, high-dimension regime, and a daily returns panel of a few thousand rows on a few hundred names is far from it — the empirical distribution is thin precisely where you most want it, and a generator will happily invent structure there. Second, generative validity is not risk validity: a model that reproduces the historical joint law well is still a model of the past, with no mechanism for a regime it has never seen. Use it to interpolate plausible scenarios between observed ones, and keep stress tests hand-built.

The twist — the deterministic route is a normalizing flow, and Z was never needed

Look again at the probability-flow ODE. It is a deterministic, invertible map from p1 = 𝒩(0,I) to p0, driven by a learned velocity field — which is the definition of a continuous normalizing flow. That identification is not decorative; it hands you three things a stochastic sampler cannot. You get exact likelihoods, via the instantaneous change-of-variables rule d(log p)/dt = −∇·vt, so that log p0(x0) = log p1(x1) + ∫01∇·vt(xt)dt along the forward ODE trajectory — a real number you can report, which the reverse SDE cannot give you. You get an encoder for free: run the ODE forwards and every sample has a unique latent, which is why latent interpolation between two generated images is coherent rather than a crossfade. And because the map is a smooth function rather than a random walk, it is distillable — a student can be trained to jump along it in one or four steps, which is how modern samplers got from a thousand network calls down to a handful. You met the same trade in Lesson 14: freeze the noise and the path becomes differentiable and reusable; leave it stochastic and it does not.

Second barrel, and it is the deeper one. Score matching sidesteps the normalizing constant entirely. Write any model as p(x) = p̃(x)/Z with p̃ unnormalized; then ∇xlog p = ∇xlog p̃ − ∇xlog Z = ∇xlog p̃, because Z does not depend on x. The score is invariant to normalization. Maximum likelihood (Lesson 3) and the cross-entropy loss (Lesson 15) cannot say that — they need log Z, and computing it is precisely what strangled the previous generation of explicit-density generative models: autoregressive models bought a tractable Z by imposing an arbitrary ordering, discrete normalizing flows bought it by restricting every layer to have a cheap Jacobian determinant, energy-based models never bought it at all and paid in MCMC. Score matching declines the purchase. That one observation — that you can learn a density's shape without ever learning its scale — is what let architectures be chosen for capacity rather than for tractability, and it is the bridge to the next lesson, where the velocity field is regressed directly and the SDE scaffolding falls away.

Why this matters · volarixs

The exact score in the panels is Σ r_k(x)·(√ᾱμ_k − x)/v_t, and rk is a posterior over which regime generated the observation. That is the same Bayes computation as the observation-update half of your regime HMM's filter: given a Gaussian emission per state, weight the states by wk𝒩(x; mk, v). The HMM adds what this toy lacks — a transition matrix, so yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior — but the update step is identical, which is why the panel's "closest centres, in √vt" reading is exactly the diagnostic that matters for regime identification too: when state means are separated by less than about one emission standard deviation, no filter can tell them apart, and a confident-looking regime label is the prior talking. Lesson 3's EM fit and this lesson's denoiser compute the same responsibilities.

Where it does not transfer, said plainly: there is no diffusion model in the Cross-Matrix (Lesson 7), and the mapping of the forward SDE onto Vasicek is a structural analogy, not a pricing result — β(t) is a design choice with no market in it, and calibrating one to anything observable is not a thing that happens. The transferable habit is smaller and real: a noise level is a resolution knob, and asking "what structure survives at √vt = x" is the same question as asking which factor structure survives a given estimation error — coarse layout resolves long before detail does, in both cases.
