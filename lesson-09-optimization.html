Lesson 09 · Wing II — the optimization engine

Gradient descent → SGD → momentum → Adam

Training is rolling downhill on a loss surface — the whole art is how you roll.

Level US Advanced undergrad → graduate FR M1–M2 · ENSAE 2A–3A

Every frontier model — and every calibration your desk runs — is trained the same way. Write down a loss: one number saying how bad the current parameters are. Compute the gradient: the arrow in parameter space along which the loss climbs fastest. Step the opposite way, scaled by a learning rate η — how far you dare to move on purely local information. That single line, w ← w − η∇f(w), is the engine. The rest of the optimizer zoo answers its two failures. First, the valley you descend is stretched — steep across, nearly flat along — and the stretch is measured by the condition number κ from Lessons 6 and 8, reborn here as a speed limit. Second, the exact gradient over a full dataset is too expensive, so you swap in a noisy estimate from a random minibatch — an estimator with variance, exactly the object of Lessons 1–3. Momentum fixes the stretch by remembering past gradients; Adam rescales each coordinate by its own gradient history; SGD lives with the noise and shrinks it by averaging. We run the whole family on an honest markets loss: the variance of a two-futures overlay, whose flat valley direction is literally Lesson 5's collinear spread.

The loss surface — a valley made of risk

The setting in plain words. You run a two-asset overlay: active weights w = (w₁, w₂) on two index futures with equal daily vol σ = 1% and return correlation ρ. The loss is the overlay's variance — hold risk, pay pain — so the risk-minimizing active book is flat, w★ = 0, and the optimizer's job is to walk any starting book down to it. (An alpha term moves w★ off zero — the second interactive does exactly that — but it never changes the shape of the valley: the Hessian stays Σ.)

  • the two active weights, in units of the book. This is parameter space: a point is a portfolio; training a model walks the same kind of space with 10⁹ coordinates.
  • covariance of the two daily returns (in %² per day). It is also the Hessian ∇²f — the curvature of the valley is the risk model itself.
  • the gradient: the vector of marginal risk contributions. The ½ in f is cosmetic — it makes the gradient exactly Σw.

The geometry in plain words. A quadratic loss is a bowl whose curvature along each principal axis is an eigenvalue of Σ (Lesson 6). For an equal-vol pair the axes sit at ±45°: the both-long direction (1,1)/√2 carries variance σ²(1+ρ) — steep; the long–short spread (1,−1)/√2 carries σ²(1−ρ) — almost nothing when the futures are near-substitutes. The bowl becomes a canyon, and its stretch is the condition number:

  • the steep axis: the market factor, both futures moving together. Largest curvature — this axis limits how big a step is safe.
  • the flat axis: the spread w₁−w₂. As ρ→1 it carries no risk — this is Lesson 5's collinear direction, and it will be the slowest to converge.
  • the condition number of Lessons 6 and 8. ρ = 0.9 → κ = 19; ρ = 0.98 → κ = 99. Watch it throttle every optimizer below.
One step downhill — and the edge of stability

Plain words first. A gradient-descent step on a quadratic is a linear map on the error: written in the eigenbasis, each component is simply multiplied by 1 − ηλᵢ every step. All of plain GD is in that number. |1−ηλᵢ| < 1 shrinks the component geometrically; |1−ηλᵢ| > 1 amplifies it geometrically. One η must serve both axes at once — that tension is the conditioning problem.

  • the learning rate (step size). The single most consequential hyperparameter in deep learning; every knob below exists to tame it.
  • error component along eigenvector eᵢ. Coordinates decouple in the eigenbasis — the optimizer is running two independent 1-D problems.
  • per-step contraction factor of axis i. Divergence happens exactly when η > 2/λ₁: the steep axis flips sign and grows. The slider below lets you cross that edge.

Why is a small step always safe? Because a gradient can only mislead you as fast as it can change. Smoothness caps that: the gradient's rate of change is at most L (for our quadratic, L = λ₁). Smoothness buys the descent lemma — a guaranteed loss decrease per step whenever η < 2/L:

  • smoothness constant: the largest curvature anywhere. For quadratics, L = λ₁ exactly; for deep nets it is the top Hessian eigenvalue, changing as you train.
  • the safety margin. Positive iff η < 2/L — the same edge as the eigen-axis picture, now derived without eigenvectors, for any smooth f.
Memory and rescaling — momentum and Adam

Momentum in plain words: the heavy ball. Keep a running velocity; feed each gradient into it. Across the canyon the gradient flips sign every step, so successive contributions cancel inside v; along the canyon they always agree, so v accumulates speed. Oscillation damped, crawl accelerated — with a single extra number per parameter.

  • momentum coefficient (0.9 throughout this lesson). Memory horizon ≈ 1/(1−β) = 10 steps of gradient history.
  • the velocity: an exponentially-weighted sum of past gradients. The "heavy ball" — inertia carries it through the zigzag.

Adam in plain words: don't just remember direction — rescale each coordinate. Track two exponential moving averages per parameter: m (the mean of recent gradients) and v (the mean of recent squared gradients). Step by m divided by √v: steep coordinates with big gradients get shrunk, flat coordinates with tiny gradients get boosted. It is a diagonal preconditioner learned on the fly — an automatic attempt to divide κ away. The full update, nothing omitted:

  • the gradient at step t (possibly a minibatch estimate). gt⊙2 squares it elementwise — each coordinate keeps its own history.
  • decay rates of the two averages (defaults 0.9 and 0.999). First moment forgets in ~10 steps; second moment in ~1000 — the step-size scale moves much more slowly than the direction.
  • EMAs of the gradient and squared gradient. m is momentum's velocity in disguise; v estimates each coordinate's gradient scale.
  • bias-corrected moments. m and v start at 0, so early EMAs underestimate; dividing by 1−β₁ᵗ and 1−β₂ᵗ (which →1 as t grows) rescales exactly the missing mass. Without it the first steps divide by a far-too-small √v̂.
  • a floor (10⁻⁸) so flat coordinates never divide by zero. Numerical guard, not mathematics.

How fast can gradient descent be?

The canyon generalizes. Strongly convex means curvature at least μ everywhere — no perfectly flat floor; smooth means at most L. For our quadratic, μ = λ₂ and L = λ₁. The best fixed step balances the two extremes: η★ = 2/(μ+L) makes the steep-axis factor and the flat-axis factor equal in size, and that shared size is the whole story of GD's speed. This is the theorem the first interactive has been acting out:

The precise statement

Let f be L-smooth and μ-strongly convex (μ > 0) with minimizer w★, and let κ = L/μ. Gradient descent wt+1 = wt − η∇f(wt) with the optimal constant step η★ = 2/(μ+L) satisfies ‖wt − w★‖ ≤ ((κ−1)/(κ+1))t ‖w₀ − w★‖. For any 0 < η < 2/L the convergence is linear with factor max{|1−ημ|, |1−ηL|}, and on quadratics these factors are attained exactly, not merely bounded. Steps-to-accuracy therefore grows like κ: conditioning is not a nuisance term — it is the rate.

For an equal-vol pair the algebra collapses: λ₁+λ₂ = 2σ², so η★ = 1/σ² exactly — independent of ρ — and (κ−1)/(κ+1) simplifies to ρ itself. The correlation of your two futures is literally optimally-tuned GD's per-step error retention. At ρ = 0.95, the best possible fixed step still keeps 95% of the remaining error every step. The gradient becomes an estimator — SGD

Plain words. So far the gradient was exact. But a training loss — and a realized portfolio variance — is an average over data: one term per day. The exact gradient costs a full pass over every day; SGD grabs B random days and averages their gradients instead. That average is a sample mean, exactly the estimator of Lessons 1–3: unbiased, with variance shrinking like 1/B. Here each day contributes the gradient of its own mean–variance objective ½(rᵗw)² − rᵗw — that day's risk leg minus that day's PnL leg:

  • the day-t return pair (in %). (rt⊤w)rt is the gradient of that day's squared PnL; −rt is the gradient of that day's alpha leg.
  • the minibatch: B days sampled with replacement from the panel. With replacement, so even B = 250 keeps some noise — a full pass would not.
  • unbiasedness: on average the minibatch gradient is the true gradient. Σ̂, μ̂ are the panel's (uncentered) covariance and mean — SGD needs no correction, only patience.
  • the estimator's covariance. The 1/B is the whole economics of batch size: quadruple B, halve the gradient noise (in RMS).

Constant η plus noise means SGD cannot settle: contraction pulls w toward w★ geometrically, noise kicks it back out, and the two balance at a stationary noise ball whose radius obeys a clean scaling law:

  • gradient-noise variance along eigen-axis i, ∝ 1/B. Computed from the panel's per-day gradients at w★; the denominator is the same 1−(contraction)² geometry as above.
  • the scaling law the demo measures. Halve η → radius ÷ √2; quadruple B → radius ÷ 2. This is why learning rates are decayed late in training.

The twist — a hard speed limit, and a workhorse without a warranty

Two facts upgrade the standard telling. First, the speed limit. Nesterov proved a lower bound: no method whose iterates are built from gradients and past gradients — GD, heavy ball, anything in that family — can beat a per-step factor of (√κ−1)/(√κ+1) on smooth strongly-convex problems. Momentum methods attain it: heavy ball on quadratics, Nesterov's accelerated gradient in general. So momentum isn't a heuristic nudge — it is optimal, and it turns κ into √κ: a canyon 100× worse-conditioned costs only 10× more steps. That exchange rate is the entire business case for momentum. Second, Adam is not a descent method. There are convex problems — the AMSGrad counterexample of Reddi, Kale & Kumar (2018) — on which Adam with standard hyperparameters provably fails to converge: its rescaling can systematically forget the rare, informative gradients. The fix (AMSGrad: never let v̂ decrease) restores the guarantee and is barely used in practice. The field's default optimizer ships with no convergence guarantee on your loss — it is used because it works, not because it is proven.

Why this matters · volarixs

Every calibration inside volarixs that fits parameters by minimizing a loss runs on this engine: smile fits, curve fits, the M-step inside the regime HMM's EM loop. Each is "gradient (or Newton) steps on an objective", and each inherits this lesson's failure modes — a stretched valley when parameters are near-redundant, a knife-edge step size, noisy gradients whenever the objective is a sample average over days.

And the canyon itself is a markets object: an ill-conditioned Σ̂ is exactly why naive mean–variance weights explode along the small-eigenvalue direction — computing Σ̂⁻¹μ̂ divides by λ₂ ≈ 0, Lesson 5's sausage again, now in weight space. That is the case for sizing risk in pca_factors space and for distrusting any weight vector that loads heavily on the spread between two near-substitutes.
