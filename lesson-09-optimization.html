Lesson 09 · Wing II — the optimization engine

Gradient descent → SGD → momentum → Adam

Training is just rolling downhill on a loss surface — the whole art is how you roll.

Level US Advanced undergrad → graduate · ML optimization FR Master M1–M2 · ENSAE 2A–3A

Every model is trained by minimizing a loss L(θ) over millions of parameters. The gradient ∇L points uphill, so you step the other way. That one idea — θ ← θ − η∇L — becomes the entire optimizer family once you ask three questions: how big a step (learning rate), do you use the whole dataset or a sample (SGD), and do you remember past gradients (momentum, Adam). Below you'll watch all three race down the same surface and see exactly why the fancy ones win.

The four updates

Gradient descent

Step downhill. Simple, but slow in stretched valleys and sensitive to η.

Momentum (heavy ball)

Accumulate velocity; cancels oscillation, accelerates on consistent slopes.

SGD

Gradient on a minibatch B — a noisy but unbiased estimate of the true gradient.

Adam — per-parameter adaptive rate

Divides each coordinate's step by its own gradient scale (√ of the running mean square) — so it self-corrects for a stretched, ill-conditioned landscape. m is momentum; s is the per-parameter variance; the hats are bias correction.

  • the gradient: steepest-ascent direction. −∇L is steepest descent, in the current coordinate metric.
  • the learning rate: step size. The single most important hyperparameter — too small crawls, too big diverges.
  • momentum coefficient and velocity. β≈0.9 keeps ~10 steps of memory.
  • Adam's second moment. Big-gradient coordinates get small steps, and vice-versa — automatic preconditioning.

The learning-rate knife-edge & SGD noise

Strip it to one dimension, L(θ)=θ². GD becomes θ ← θ(1−2η): it converges only while |1−2η| < 1. Past that it oscillates, then explodes. And a minibatch adds gradient noise, so SGD can't settle exactly — it hovers in a noise floor around the minimum.

The precise statement

Gradient descent θ_{t+1} = θ_t − η∇L(θ_t) converges on a smooth convex L for η < 2/L_smooth; on a quadratic with Hessian condition number κ, plain GD's rate degrades like (κ−1)/(κ+1) per step — slow when κ is large. Momentum improves the dependence to roughly √κ. SGD replaces ∇L with an unbiased minibatch estimate whose variance sets a noise floor unless η decays. Adam rescales each coordinate by its running RMS gradient, approximating a diagonal preconditioner that removes much of the κ-dependence.

The twist — the valley is collinearity, and non-convexity mostly doesn't bite

The stretched bowl that makes GD zig-zag is an ill-conditioned Hessian — the exact κ = λ_max/λ_min from Lessons 5–6 and 8, now in parameter space. That's why plain GD staggers and why Adam (a diagonal preconditioner) and momentum (√κ acceleration) win: they're fighting the same conditioning problem you met in regression. Two deeper surprises hold in real training. First, the loss of a deep net is wildly non-convex — countless minima and far more saddle points — yet SGD reliably finds good minima, because in high dimensions almost all critical points are saddles (escapable) and most minima generalize comparably; its noise even helps escape sharp ones. Second, the learning rate sits on a genuine knife-edge (the "edge of stability"), which is why LR schedules and warmup exist. Optimization theory here is less "find the global min" and more "the geometry is forgiving if you precondition and add a little noise."

Why this matters · volarixs

Every gradient-trained model you run — the XGBoost objective's Newton steps, any neural forecaster, a logistic signal — is doing exactly this descent, almost always with Adam. The reason Adam is the default is the twist above: your features are correlated, so the loss Hessian is ill-conditioned, and a plain fixed learning rate would crawl down the narrow valley — Adam's per-parameter rescaling is preconditioning that collinearity away.

Two carry-overs: the minibatch gradient is an estimator (Lessons 1–3) — its variance is why small batches train noisier and why the learning rate and batch size trade off; and if a model won't converge, suspect the learning rate first (the knife-edge) before the architecture. Preconditioning, warmup, and LR schedules are all fighting conditioning, not a bug in your code.
