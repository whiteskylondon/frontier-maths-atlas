Lesson 17 · Wing IV — inside the transformer

Scaled dot-product attention — softmax, Q/K/V mechanics

Every token asks a question, every token advertises what it holds, and the answer is a probability-weighted average of payloads — kernel regression with a learned metric.

Level US Graduate · ML FR M2 · ENSAE 3A

Attention is a soft dictionary lookup. A hard dictionary takes a query, finds the one key that matches, and returns that key's value. Attention softens every step: the query is compared to all keys at once by inner product (Lesson 6: inner product = similarity), the softmax turns those similarity scores into a probability row, and the output is the probability-weighted average of all the values. Nothing more is happening inside the layer that reads this page's tokens — but because every step is smooth, the whole lookup is differentiable end to end, so gradient descent (Lesson 13) can learn what to ask, what to advertise, and what to hand back.

The vocabulary, in plain words
  • querythe question a token asks: "who out there is relevant to me?" — a vector. In this lesson's market toy: today's state, summarized as (vol level, trend).
  • keywhat each token advertises: "this is what I hold." Each historical day's own (vol, trend) state.
  • valuethe payload actually retrieved when a key wins attention. That historical day's next-day return — what happened after the market looked like that.
  • scorequery·key — one similarity number per (query, key) pair. Lesson 6's inner product; Lesson 10 told us how such numbers behave in high dimension, and that fact returns below as the √dk.
  • softmaxthe machine that turns any row of scores into positive weights summing to 1 — a probability row. Exponentiate, then normalize. Big scores win disproportionately.
  • temperaturethe sharpness dial τ: scores are divided by τ before the softmax. Small τ → winner-take-all; large τ → everyone-equal. Attention hard-wires τ = √dk, and proving why is this lesson's centrepiece.
  • headone complete query–key–value lookup. A transformer runs many in parallel, each with its own learned notion of "similar".
  • causal maskthe rule "no looking at the future": scores of not-yet-seen tokens are set to −∞, so after the softmax their weights are exactly 0.
The machinery — one line
  • the queries, n×dk — one row per token: what each position is looking for.
  • the score matrix S, n×n: Sij = ⟨qi, kj⟩. In einsum this is nd,md->nm (Lesson 11). Lesson 11's twist, restated: softmax normalizes each row of S, and that nonlinearity blocks reassociation — the n×n intermediate must exist, so one head costs O(n²dk). At n = 4,096 that is 4,096² = 16,777,216 scores per head.
  • the one magic constant, proved two sections down: it pins the score standard deviation at 1 at every head width.
  • applied row-wise: each row of scores becomes a probability row wi· — non-negative, summing to 1, a point on the simplex. Shift-invariant: adding a constant to a whole row changes nothing. That is why −∞ masking and subtract-the-max numerics are both clean.
  • the values, n×dv — the payloads. Output row i is Σj wijvj: a convex combination (Lesson 14) of value rows.

The softmax deserves its own line, because the temperature reading of the √dk is the key to everything below:

  • a row of n scores, one per key.
  • temperature. Dividing by small τ stretches score gaps → the weight row's entropy (Lesson 15) falls toward 0 bits; large τ erases gaps → entropy rises toward log₂n bits. Attention is softmax at temperature τ = √dk applied to raw dot products.
  • why the exponential? It is the choice that makes weights depend only on score differences — and it is the Gibbs distribution of statistical mechanics with energy −si, which is where the word "temperature" comes from.

The magic constant — why √dk

Take a query and a key drawn independently of each other, each with independent components of mean 0 and variance 1 — roughly what a normalization layer hands the attention block (LayerNorm rescales every token vector to zero mean and unit variance before it is projected; that is Lesson 19's business). Their dot product is then a sum of dk independent zero-mean products, so the variances add:

  • one product term: mean 0, and variance E[qi²]·E[ki²] = 1·1 = 1 by independence.
  • the whole one-line proof: dk independent unit-variance terms ⇒ variance dk, std √dk. Raw scores grow with width: std 2 at d = 4, std 8 at d = 64, std 32 at d = 1024. This is Lesson 10's concentration read backwards: cosines shrink like 1/√d exactly because raw inner products grow like √d while the norms grow like √d each.
  • dividing a random variable by c divides its std by c — so scores/√dk have std 1 at every width. One constant, and softmax sees the same score scale whether the head is 4-dimensional or 4,096-dimensional.

Why saturation kills learning: at dk = 64 (GPT-2's head width) unscaled scores have std 8, so a routine 2-σ gap between the top two scores is 16 — and softmax turns a gap of 16 into a weight ratio of e¹⁶ ≈ 8.9 million. The row is one-hot, one-hot rows have (near-)zero gradient through the softmax, and nothing upstream learns. Many heads, and no peeking
  • the token stream, n×dmodel — every head reads the same input.
  • head h's learned projections into its own dk = dmodel/H dimensional subspace. Head h's score is ⟨WhQx, WhKx′⟩ — its own metric: one head can score "similar trend" while a sibling scores "similar vol". Splitting dmodel keeps the total cost equal to one full-width head.
  • concatenate the H verdicts and mix them back to n×dmodel with one learned matrix.
  • maskcausal attention sets Sij ← −∞ for j > i before the softmax. Then e−∞ = 0: future tokens get exactly zero weight and each row renormalizes over positions ≤ i — still a probability row, now over the past only. Masking after the softmax would break sum-to-1; before, it is exact.

The precise statement

Let Q ∈ ℝn×dk, K ∈ ℝm×dk, V ∈ ℝm×dv and W = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk) row-wise. (i) Every row of W lies on the simplex Δm−1, so each output row (WV)i = Σj wijvj is a convex combination of the rows of V: attention outputs live in the convex hull of the values — the layer interpolates, never extrapolates. (ii) If q, k ∈ ℝdk are independent of each other and each has independent components of mean 0 and variance 1, then E[qᵀk] = 0 and Var(qᵀk) = dk; hence scores divided by √dk have variance exactly 1 for every dk. (iii) For any m×m permutation matrix P, softmax(Q(PK)ᵀ/√dk)(PV) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk)V: jointly permuting key/value rows changes nothing but the labels. Unmasked attention is a set operation — token order is invisible to it and must be injected separately (Lesson 18).

The twist — sixty-year-old statistics wearing new weights

Freeze the metric and attention is exactly Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression (1964): m̂(x) = Σᵢ K(x,xᵢ)yᵢ / Σⱼ K(x,xⱼ) — the analog-days panel above is the estimator every nonparametric statistics course teaches, with kernel K(x,x′) = exp(⟨W_Q x, W_K x′⟩/√d_k). The revolution is not the estimator. It is that the metric is learned: WQ, WK are trained end-to-end by gradient descent (Lesson 13), jointly across dozens of layers and heads, so each head discovers which notion of "similar" makes its predictions cheap — something no 1964 statistician could fit. Second barrel: the theorem's convexity is a cage. A head's output can never leave the convex hull of its values, so a stack of pure attention layers can only re-mix what the dictionary already contains. Breaking out of the hull is the MLP block's job — and how attention and MLPs share one residual stream is Lesson 19.

Why this matters · volarixs

A similarity-weighted "analog days" forecaster over regime features is, literally, one attention head with a hand-chosen metric: today's feature vector is the query, history's are the keys, next-day outcomes are the values — Interactive 1 is exactly that estimator, and the temperature slider is the dial between nearest-neighbour brittleness and global-mean blandness. If volarixs ever scores "days like today", this is its mathematics.

The regime HMM already runs softmax weights: its filtered state probabilities are a softmax over per-state scores (log prior + log likelihood) — a soft lookup over regimes instead of days. And the convexity theorem is a risk statement: any such forecaster is bounded by the best and worst outcomes in its own history. It will never predict a move it has never seen — stress scenarios must come from somewhere else.
