Lesson 05 · Wing 0 — statistical & linear-algebra foundations

Least squares in matrix form: XᵀX, the normal equations, and ridge

One orthogonality condition turns a factor fit into a matrix equation — and the eigenvalues of XᵀX decide whether you can trust the answer.

Level US Graduate · ML FR M2 · ENSAE 3A

A trading book made or lost money on each of 250 days; three published factor indices — market, value, momentum — also moved each day. The cleanest question a risk desk can ask: how much of the book is each factor? Stack the factor returns into a table, one row per day, one column per factor plus a column of ones for the standing drift — that table is the design matrix X ("design" because you chose its columns). The book's returns stack into a vector y. A beta is a sensitivity — how many units of book return arrive per unit of factor return — and the hat on β̂ means "estimated from data" rather than known. Least squares picks the betas that make the leftover part of y — the residual — as small as possible in the sum-of-squares sense. Lesson 4 already told us what that leftover must look like: the closest point in a subspace leaves a remainder orthogonal to it. Today that geometry becomes a matrix equation, and one k×k object — XᵀX — turns out to control the answer's existence, its stability, and its error bars.

The words, before any formula
  • design matrix — the n×k spreadsheet of explanatory data: n = 250 rows (days), k = 4 columns (ones, MKT, VAL, MOM). Everything below is about the geometry of its four columns as vectors in 250-dimensional day-space.
  • response — the book's daily returns; ŷ is the model's reconstruction of them. ŷ must lie in the span of X's columns; y almost never does.
  • loadings / hedge ratios — the sensitivities; β̂ is their least-squares estimate. β̂ is what the desk actually trades on: it says how much index future to short per unit of book.
  • residual r = y − Xβ̂ — the part no factor explains; the alpha candidate. Least squares makes it orthogonal to every factor by construction.
  • Gram matrix — the k×k table of dot products between columns: how big each factor is and how much any two overlap. Collinearity, conditioning, error bars: all of it is stored here.
  • condition number — λmax/λmin of XᵀX: how uneven the information is across directions of β-space. Large κ = some combination of betas is nearly unmeasured.
  • variance inflation factor — the multiplier that collinearity applies to one beta's variance. Quantified below; it is the number that tells you whether individual loadings are trustworthy.
The setup — one book, three factors

The page simulates the panel with truths fixed so you can watch the estimator work: α = 1 bp/day of true alpha, β = (1.0, 0.4, −0.3) on market, value, momentum; factor vols 1.0%, 0.6%, 0.6% a day; idiosyncratic noise σ = 0.5%/day; n = 250 days, seeded and reproducible.

  • book returns, an n-vector (units: %/day). One year of daily P&L, de-levered.
  • columns (1, MKT, VAL, MOM) — the ones column carries the intercept α. Forgetting it forces the fit through zero and biases every beta.
  • the k-vector (α, βMKT, βVAL, βMOM). Truth here: (0.01, 1.0, 0.4, −0.3) in %/day units — α is the 0.01.
  • idiosyncratic noise, E[ε]=0, Var(ε)=σ²Iₙ. The Gauss–Markov assumptions live entirely in this term.
One orthogonality condition → the normal equations

Lesson 4: the closest point to y inside a subspace is its orthogonal projection, and the leftover is perpendicular to the whole subspace. Here the subspace is span(x₁,…,x₄) — the four columns. So the optimal residual must have zero dot product with each column. Stack those k statements into one matrix line and solve:

  • the k dot products ⟨xⱼ, r⟩ stacked into one vector — orthogonality, columnwise. Interactive 2 below computes them and shows they vanish to machine precision, not approximately.
  • the normal equations — k linear equations in k unknowns. "Normal" as in perpendicular. The calculus route agrees: ∇β‖y−Xβ‖² = −2Xᵀ(y−Xβ), and zeroing the gradient is the same condition.
  • exists ⟺ the columns of X are linearly independent (full column rank). Two identical factors, or k > n, and OLS simply has no unique answer.
See it — the conditioning experiment

Now make value and momentum returns correlated at level ρ and refit. The damage to each collinear beta is exactly quantified by the variance inflation factor:

  • the R² from regressing column j on the other columns — how predictable this factor is from the rest. In this panel MKT is independent, so R² for VAL is just ρ² against MOM.
  • the standard error the beta would have if the factors were orthogonal. At ρ = 0.95: VIF = 1/(1−0.95²) ≈ 10.3, so each collinear SE is √10.3 ≈ 3.2× wider. Pure formula — the panel below confirms it empirically.

The hat matrix — Lesson 4's projection, computable

Substitute β̂ back into ŷ = Xβ̂ and the whole fit collapses into a single matrix acting on y. It is called the hat matrix because it literally puts the hat on y — and it is precisely the orthogonal projector of Lesson 4, now something you can print:

  • the orthogonal projector onto col(X) — n×n, built only from the factors. ŷ = Py: the fit is a linear function of y before you ever see y.
  • idempotent — projecting twice is projecting once: once you're in the subspace, you stay. With Pᵀ = P this forces every eigenvalue to be 0 or 1 — a projector has no other options.
  • the eigenvalue-1 count = dimensions absorbed by the fit — here 4. This trace is the "degrees of freedom used", the k in the n−k below.
  • the residual-maker: r = (I−P)y, the projector onto the orthogonal complement. Also idempotent, with trace n−k — the room the residual lives in.

Precision — Gauss–Markov and σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹

How good is β̂? Under the model's noise assumptions the answer is one matrix formula — and estimating σ² honestly reuses Lesson 2's degrees-of-freedom story, with k fitted directions eaten instead of one:

  • the noise variance — one number, but it multiplies a whole matrix. XᵀX/σ² is exactly the Fisher information for β in the Gaussian model — Lesson 3 in matrix form.
  • the geometry of precision: diagonal entries give each SE², off-diagonals the estimation covariances. Along a unit eigenvector with eigenvalue λ the variance is σ²/λ — small λ, huge doubt. This is where the sausage above comes from.
  • residual degrees of freedom — 250 − 4 = 246 here. The residual is trapped in an (n−k)-dimensional subspace, so dividing by n would understate σ². Lesson 2's n−1 is the k=1, intercept-only case.

One sobering constant from this formula: SE(α̂) ≈ σ/√n = 0.5%/√250 ≈ 3.2 bp/day. The simulated book's true alpha is 1 bp — a year of daily data cannot distinguish it from zero. Betas fare better only because factor vol amplifies their signal.

The precise statement

Let y = Xβ + ε with E[ε] = 0, Var(ε) = σ²Iₙ, and X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵏ of full column rank. Then ‖y − Xβ‖² is strictly convex with unique minimiser β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, characterised by Xᵀ(y − Xβ̂) = 0. The fit ŷ = Py is the orthogonal projection of y onto col(X), where P = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ is symmetric and idempotent with tr(P) = k. The estimator is unbiased, E[β̂] = β, with Var(β̂) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹, and among all linear unbiased estimators it has minimal variance (Gauss–Markov). Finally σ̂² = ‖y − Xβ̂‖²/(n−k) is unbiased for σ².

The twist — a tiny eigenvalue is a flat direction of the loss

Diagonalise XᵀX. Along a unit eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ, the RSS curves like λ·t² — and Var(vᵀβ̂) = σ²/λ. A tiny λ therefore means a nearly flat valley in the loss: you can slide β̂ far along v and the fit barely changes, so the data barely pins it down. That is what ρ = 0.95 does above: the difference direction (β̂VAL − β̂MOM) gets the small eigenvalue and inflates ~10× in variance, while the sum direction keeps a large eigenvalue and stays pinned — individual betas become garbage while β̂VAL+β̂MOM and the fitted values ŷ remain perfectly estimable. The repair is spectral surgery, not more data: ridge replaces XᵀX by XᵀX+λI (every eigenvalue lifted, nothing near zero), and Lesson 8's truncated pseudoinverse simply refuses to divide by the small σ² directions. Both trade a little bias for the variance you just watched explode.

Why this matters · volarixs

A factor regression of book returns on drivers is this object: β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy are the hedge ratios, ŷ = Py the factor-explained P&L, and alpha is the residual (I−P)y — orthogonal to the drivers by construction, straight from Lesson 4. When drivers are collinear (value/quality, two vol tenors), XᵀX is ill-conditioned and the hedge ratios slide along the sausage: signs flip refit to refit, which is precisely the pathology sign_stability is built to flag before a ratio goes into production.

And pca_factors diagonalises exactly this matrix — XᵀX on the centred returns panel is the covariance being eigendecomposed. Its small eigenvalues are the untrustworthy directions of any regression run on those drivers, so the same spectrum doubles as a conditioning diagnostic: κ(XᵀX) before trusting loadings, and Lesson 8's numerical-rank read ("count the non-negligible eigenvalues") as the honest number of independent drivers in the book.
