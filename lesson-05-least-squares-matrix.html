Least squares in matrix form: XᵀX, the normal equations, and ridge

One gradient turns fitting a model into a matrix equation — and XᵀX runs the show.

Level US Graduate · ML / linear models FR Master M2 · ENSAE 3A

Stack the data: an n×p design matrix X (rows = observations, columns = features) and a response vector y. A linear model predicts ŷ = Xβ, and least squares asks for the β minimising the squared error ‖y − Xβ‖². Differentiate once and the answer collapses to a single matrix equation whose entire behaviour — stability, precision, when it fails — is governed by the p×p Gram matrix XᵀX.

The precise statement

When X has full column rank, RSS is strictly convex and β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy is its unique global minimiser; ŷ = Hy is the orthogonal projection of y onto col(X), with residual orthogonal to every column. The estimator's variance is Var(β̂) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹, so small eigenvalues of XᵀX mean large coefficient variance. Ridge, β̂λ = (XᵀX+λI)⁻¹Xᵀy, is defined for every λ>0 — even when XᵀX is singular.

The twist — XᵀX's eigenvalues are the whole story

Because Var(β̂) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹, a single tiny eigenvalue of XᵀX — the signature of near-collinear features — inflates coefficient variance without bound: the estimate becomes a wild, unreliable number that flips sign from sample to sample. Ridge adds λ to every eigenvalue, so (XᵀX+λI) can't be singular and the small directions stop exploding — you accept a little bias to buy a lot of variance back, the same shrinkage trade as Lessons 2 and 3. Two connections worth carrying: XᵀX/σ² is exactly the Fisher information for β in the Gaussian model, so "well-conditioned XᵀX" literally means "lots of information, tight coefficients" — Lesson 3 in matrix form. And when p > n, XᵀX is singular and OLS simply does not exist; regularisation isn't a refinement, it's the only way to get an answer.

Why this matters · volarixs

A cross-sectional factor regression is this object: returns on a design matrix of factor exposures, β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy the loadings, ŷ = Hy the factor-explained return, and your alpha the residual (I−H)y — orthogonal to the factors by construction, straight from Lesson 4. When factors are correlated (value/quality, size/liquidity), XᵀX is ill-conditioned and loadings flip sign across samples — the coefficient cloud above, in your panel.

Two operational takeaways: the condition number of XᵀX is a diagnostic you can compute on any factor panel to know whether the loadings are trustworthy before you act on them; and with many signals on limited history you drift toward p > n, where OLS is undefined and ridge (or its cousins — lasso, elastic net, the same Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage spirit) is mandatory, not optional.
