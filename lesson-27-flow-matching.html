Lesson 27 · Wing VI — generative machinery

Flow matching & continuous normalizing flows

Stop learning how to undo noise. Learn the wind that blows noise into data.

LevelUS GraduateFR M2 – PhD

Lesson 26 built a generator by running a noising process backwards. Flow matching throws that framing away and asks a blunter question: if I want to carry a cloud of pure noise onto a cloud of data, what velocity should a particle at position x at time t be travelling at? Learn that velocity field, then release particles from noise and integrate an ordinary differential equation forwards. No stochastic process, no score function, no simulation inside the training loop. The obstacle is that the velocity you want — the one that transports the whole cloud — is an integral you cannot compute. The resolution is a theorem so old it predates all of this: if you regress on a random target, least squares hands you its conditional mean for free. That is Lesson 4's projection theorem, and it is the entire reason modern generative models can be trained at all.

The words, before any symbols
  • velocity fielda rule v(x,t) that assigns an arrow to every position x at every time t: "a particle sitting here, now, should move this way, this fast". It is a function, not a trajectory. Trajectories are what you get by following it.
  • flowthe map you obtain by starting a particle at x₀ and following the arrows from t = 0 to t = 1. Deterministic and invertible — run the arrows backwards and you return to where you started. A continuous normalizing flow is exactly this, used as a generative model.
  • probability patha schedule of distributions pt, one for each time, starting at noise p₀ and ending at the data distribution p₁ = q. You choose it. This is the design freedom the whole lesson is about.
  • divergence ∇·vhow much the arrows spread apart at a point: the sum of the diagonal entries of the Jacobian ∂v/∂x. Positive means the flow is stretching space there (density thins); negative means it is squeezing (density piles up). The Jacobian is Lesson 13's object; the divergence is its trace.
  • couplingthe rule that decides which noise point x₀ is paired with which data point x₁ when you build a training example. Drawing them independently is the default. It is not the only choice, and the choice matters — see the twist.
  • marginal vs conditional"conditional" = the velocity that moves one particular pair (x₀, x₁) along its own private path. "Marginal" = the single field that correctly transports the whole cloud. You can write down the first. You cannot write down the second. Bridging that gap is the lesson.
  • Euler stepthe crudest ODE integrator: x ← x + h·v(x,t), then t ← t + h. One network evaluation per step. The number of steps you need is the sampling cost of the model — the quantity everyone is fighting over.
A flow, and what it does to density

Fix a velocity field v and push every point along it. Two equations describe what happens — one for a particle, one for the density it carries:

  • one particle's position at time t. Here d = 2 so you can watch it; in an image model d is a hundred thousand.
  • the density of the whole cloud at time t. The middle equation is the continuity equation — mass is conserved, it only moves. It is the statement "v generates pt".
  • the divergence: the trace of the d×d Jacobian of v with respect to x. Sum of ∂v₁/∂x₁ + … + ∂v_d/∂x_d.
  • the instantaneous change of variables: ride along with the particle and watch its own log-density change. The discrete-flow analogue is "subtract log|det J| at every layer"; here the determinant becomes a trace and the layers become time.

That third identity is what made continuous normalizing flows attractive in 2018 and what made them nearly untrainable. Exact likelihood needs log p₁, which means simulating the ODE inside every training step and accumulating ∇·v along it — and for large d even one divergence is a d-fold Jacobian trace, so practice fell back on a stochastic (Hutchinson) trace estimate built from the vector-Jacobian products of Lesson 13. Slow, noisy, and fragile. Flow matching's entire contribution is that it deletes both costs: no simulation, no divergence, at training time.

The objective you want, and the one you can compute

Suppose someone hands you the target field ut(x) — the true marginal velocity that transports p₀ to q. Then training a network vθ would be plain regression:

  • the network, taking a position and a time, returning a vector in ℝd. θ are its weights; ∇θ below always means the gradient in those weights.
  • the marginal velocity — the one field that moves the entire cloud correctly. Unavailable: writing it down requires an integral over the whole data distribution, at every x and every t.

Now condition. Pick a latent z — a data point x₁, or a whole noise/data pair (x₀,x₁) — and build a path pt(·|z) whose velocity ut(x|z) you can write in closed form, such that averaging the conditional paths over q gives the marginal path. Regress on that instead:

  • the conditional velocity: elementary, exact, and computable from one sampled z. This is the label the network actually sees. It is a random label — the same (x,t) arrives with different targets on different draws.
  • the conditioning latent. z = x₁ recovers Lipman et al.'s form; z = (x₀,x₁) recovers the rectified-flow / OT-coupling form. Both satisfy the theorem below.

The two losses are not equal. Their gradients are, and the reason is a single line you already own from Lesson 4: the minimiser of a squared error against a random target is the conditional mean of that target. Here the conditioning event is "the particle is at x at time t":

  • the conditional expectation of Lesson 4 — the L² projection onto functions of the conditioning variable. Posterior weights over z, normalised by pt(x): the denominator is the tower property doing bookkeeping.
  • gradient in the network's weights. The two losses differ by 𝔼‖ut(x|z) − ut(x)‖², a number that does not contain θ — so it vanishes under ∇θ. That constant is the irreducible noise floor of the training loss, not an error.

Read that in desk language: no single training example ever shows the network the right answer. Each shows one noisy draw whose average, over everything the network cannot see, is the right answer — and squared-error regression is precisely the machine that extracts that average.

Choosing the path — straight lines against the diffusion arc

Take Gaussian conditional paths, the family that covers both frameworks. Fix a schedule (αt, σt) with α₀ ≈ 0, σ₀ ≈ 1 (noise) and α₁ = 1, σ₁ = 0 (data), and set pt(x|x₁) = 𝒩(αtx₁, σt²I). Sampling it means drawing x₀ ∼ 𝒩(0,I) and forming xt:

  • the schedule: how much data and how much noise at time t. Dots mean d/dt. Note that xt = αtx₁ + σtx₀ is exactly Lesson 14's reparameterization — the noise x₀ is frozen and everything else is deterministic, which is what lets you differentiate the loss at all. The two conditional velocities above are the same random variable given xt = x: substitute x₀ = (x − αtx₁)/σt.
  • the linear / optimal-transport path. Then ut(x|x₀,x₁) = x₁ − x₀ exactly: a constant. The conditional trajectory is the straight segment from x₀ to x₁ at constant speed, and its kinetic energy ∫₀¹‖u‖²dt is exactly ‖x₁ − x₀‖² — the squared-distance cost optimal transport minimises. Hence the name; this is also the rectified-flow path.
  • the variance-preserving diffusion path of Lesson 26, where β is that lesson's noise-rate schedule — taken linear from 0.1 to 20 — T(s) = ∫₀ˢβ(r)dr, and σt = √(1−αt²). Two honest details. α₀ = — rather than 0 — the well-known, small VP mismatch: the path only approaches pure noise. And σ̇t ∼ −(1−t)−1/2 as t → 1, so its conditional paths have finite length but infinite kinetic energy. There is no transport cost to quote for it.

And the bridge back to Lesson 26. For any Gaussian path, the score ∇x log pt(x) — the object score matching learns — and the marginal velocity are the same information in different coordinates, related by an affine map with time-dependent coefficients:

  • the score — the gradient in x of the log-density. Not Lesson 14's score, which is the gradient in the parameters. Same word, different derivative; keep them apart.
  • the conversion coefficient multiplying the score; the relation inverts wherever it is finite and non-zero — that is, αt > 0 and σt > 0. Substitute the VP schedule and it collapses to ut(x) = ½β(1−t)[x + ∇x log pt(x)] — Lesson 26's probability-flow ODE exactly, with the sign flipped because L26 runs time data → noise and this lesson runs noise → data; mind that when you move between the two. So a score model is a velocity model wearing different clothes, and what flow matching adds is the freedom to choose a different (αt, σt) altogether.

The precise statement

(i) Instantaneous change of variables. Let v(·,t) be continuously differentiable in x, uniformly Lipschitz on compacts, and let xt solve ẋt = v(xt,t) from x₀ ∼ p₀. Then the law pt of xt satisfies the continuity equation ∂tpt + ∇·(ptv) = 0, and along any trajectory d/dt log pt(xt) = −∇·v(xt,t) = −tr(∂v/∂x) (Chen et al., 2018).

(ii) Marginalisation and gradient equality. Let q be the data distribution and, for q-almost every latent z, let ut(·|z) generate pt(·|z), with pt(x) = ∫pt(x|z)q(z)dz > 0 and the integrands bounded and integrable. Define ut(x) = ∫ut(x|z)pt(x|z)q(z)dz / pt(x), which is exactly E[ut(x|z) | xt = x]. Then ut generates the marginal path pt, and ℒFM(θ) = ℒCFM(θ) − c with c = 𝔼‖ut(x|z) − ut(x)‖² independent of θ; hence ∇θℒFM = ∇θℒCFM whenever both are finite (Lipman et al., 2023, for z = x₁; the same argument with z = (x₀,x₁) gives the rectified-flow form).

(iii) The linear path. For αt = t, σt = 1−t and xt = (1−t)x₀ + tx₁, the conditional velocity is ẋt = x₁ − x₀, constant in t; equivalently ut(x|x₁) = (x₁ − x)/(1−t) for t < 1. Every conditional trajectory is a straight segment. This does not imply that the marginal field's trajectories are straight — that depends on the coupling of x₀ to x₁.

The twist — a 1930s theorem is holding up the whole building

Strip flow matching to its load-bearing beam and it reads: regress on a random target, receive its conditional mean. That is the L² projection theorem — Kolmogorov-era probability, the same fact that makes a regression coefficient a hedge ratio in Lesson 4. Simulation-free training of every modern continuous generative model rests on it. Nothing about the trick is neural; the network is only the function class in which the projection is carried out.

Second barrel, and it is sharper than it looks. The theorem constrains only the marginal field, so the coupling between noise and data is yours to choose — draw the pair independently, or pair them cleverly, and both are valid flow-matching objectives that generate the same q. On this page's 600 seeded pairs, replacing the independent coupling with minibatch optimal transport (exact assignment inside batches of six) cuts the mean transport cost from — to — and the number of crossing pairs — counted within each batch of six and summed over all hundred of them — from — to —, while changing neither endpoint marginal, since it is only a permutation. What it does change is the field: a different coupling means a different pt in between, and so a different ut to learn. And this matters because of the fact the first interactive measures: the linear path's training trajectories are perfectly straight (arc ÷ chord = —) yet under an independent coupling its sampling trajectories are the more curved of the two (— against the diffusion path's —), because independently-paired straight lines cross, and the marginal field has to average the crossing. Straight conditional paths are not a straight flow. Rectified flow closes the gap by iterating — sample the flow, re-pair each noise point with the endpoint it actually reached, refit — and each pass provably does not increase the transport cost while straightening the field.

Re-applied — the regression you already trust

The mechanism is not new to you, and it is not new to finance. Price an American option by Longstaff–Schwartz: at each exercise date you need the continuation value, E[discounted future payoff | today's state] — and you cannot compute it. What you can do is simulate one path forward, record the single realised discounted payoff, and regress that scrappy number on functions of the current state. The realised payoff is a hopeless predictor of itself; the regression does not care, because least squares returns the conditional mean of whatever target you feed it. Same for a minimum-variance hedge ratio, a factor beta, a fitted expected shortfall: in every case the label is one draw and the estimand is its conditional expectation. Flow matching is that scheme applied to velocity fields — an intractable conditional expectation replaced by a computable random draw whose mean is the thing you wanted, and the only novelty is what sits in the regressand.

Why this matters · volarixs

The transferable part is not the generative model — the platform has no path-generator, and pretending otherwise would be inventing a connection. It is the target-variance argument. Every Cross-Matrix cell is Lesson 4's projection fitted on a target you only ever observe as one realisation, so its estimand is fixed by the conditioning and its precision is set by the conditional variance of the label. Flow matching's second half is the observation that the label's variance is a design variable: the OT coupling above keeps both endpoint marginals, so the model still generates the same q, while shrinking the spread of the target the regression sees. Be exact about what survives, though — re-pairing leaves the endpoints alone but does change the intermediate field, which is precisely what rectified flow exploits; it buys variance by moving to a different, straighter estimand, not by holding the old one fixed.

Operationally that is the question to ask of any cell whose sign_stability is marginal: before more history or more shrinkage, is there a re-pairing or a control variate that leaves the estimand alone and cuts the label's variance — differencing against a sector or index leg, matching on a covariate, using an overlapping-window construction with the right correction? The honest boundary: a variance reduction that also moves the conditional mean is not a variance reduction, it is a different estimator, and the burden is on you to show which one you built.
