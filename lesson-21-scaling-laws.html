Lesson 21 · Wing V — training-dynamics mysteries

Scaling laws — power laws & compute-optimal training

Test loss falls in a straight line on log-log paper. The only decision left is how to spend the budget.

Level US Graduate FR Master 2 (M2)

Train a language model and its test loss — the cross-entropy of Lesson 15, in nats per token — falls in a way that is embarrassingly regular. Plot loss against model size on paper where both axes are logarithmic and you get a straight line, holding across five or more factors of ten. A straight line in logs is a power law: loss times a constant factor for every multiplication of size. That regularity is what makes frontier training a planning exercise rather than a gamble — you can extrapolate a run you have not done. It also forces one sharp question, because compute is the thing you actually buy: with a fixed budget of arithmetic, do you train a big model on few tokens or a small model on many? Two papers gave opposite answers two years apart, and one of them was wrong for reasons that had nothing to do with scaling. Underneath both sits a floor the straight line can never cross: the entropy of the data itself.

The words, before the machinery
  • power lawa relation y = k·x−p: multiply x by 10 and y is multiplied by 10−p, whatever x you started from. Scale-free — there is no special size, no knee. Taking logs turns it into a straight line of slope −p, which is why every plot in this lesson is log-log.
  • test lossthe model's average cross-entropy on data it never saw, in nats per token — natural-log units of surprise; divide by ln 2 ≈ 0.693 to read them as bits. Exactly Lesson 15's H(p,q) = H(p) + KL(p‖q): a floor set by the world plus a penalty that is yours.
  • N, D, CN = parameters, D = training tokens, C = compute in FLOPs (floating-point operations) spent on the run. The three axes of the whole literature. C is the one with a price tag; N and D are how you spend it.
  • iso-computethe set of (N, D) pairs costing the same C — a hyperbola, since cost is roughly proportional to N·D. Walking along it is the trade: every parameter you add is tokens you must give up.
  • compute-optimalthe point on that curve with the lowest loss. Not "the biggest model you can fit in memory" — the biggest model is usually the wrong answer, which is the punchline of the lesson.
  • floor Ethe irreducible part of the loss: what remains as N and D go to infinity. The data's own entropy — the surprise no model can price away (Lesson 15). Everything above it is called the reducible loss, L − E.
  • tokens/paramthe ratio D/N at your chosen allocation. The single number the Chinchilla paper is remembered for ("about 20"), and the number this lesson will make you distrust slightly.
A power law is a straight line in logs

The first systematic study — Kaplan et al. 2020 (OpenAI, Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models) — fitted transformer loss against each axis separately, with the other axis held generous, and got pure power laws with no additive floor:

  • the exponents — the slopes of the straight lines, and the only numbers that matter for extrapolation. 0.076 and 0.095 are shockingly small: a 10× bigger model buys a factor 10−0.076 ≈ 0.84 in loss under this fit.
  • the scale constants — where each line crosses L = 1. Units-carrying nuisance parameters; nobody quotes them because they change with tokenizer, architecture and data. Kaplan's N counts non-embedding parameters only.
  • the log-log form: linear in ln N with slope −αN. This is why "scaling law" and "straight line" are used interchangeably — and why a bend in the line is evidence of a floor, not of a broken law.

Kaplan's headline recommendation was: as the budget grows, spend it on size. Their compute-optimal allocation was N ∝ C0.73 and D ∝ C0.27 — the exponents must sum to 1, since C is proportional to N·D, and 0.73 versus 0.27 says parameters nearly three times as fast as data. The industry believed it, and built accordingly. Flag one thing now, because the argument turns on it later: that 0.73 does not follow from the two fits just quoted. It came from a separate analysis in the same paper, phrased in optimization steps rather than tokens.

What a training run costs — the 6ND rule

Before optimizing, price the thing. One token's forward pass touches each weight once in a multiply-accumulate — Lesson 12's counting unit — and a fused multiply-add is 2 floating-point operations. The backward pass of Lesson 13 costs about twice the forward, because reverse-mode has to build both the gradient flowing to the inputs and the gradient landing on the weights. So:

  • FLOPs per parameter per token in the forward pass: one multiply, one add. Lesson 12 counted multiply-adds because only multiplications recurse; here we count both halves, because we are buying hardware seconds.
  • the forward + backward multiplier: 1 for the forward, ≈2 for the reverse sweep. Lesson 13's cheap-gradient principle bounds the adjoint pass at a small constant times the primal — Baur–Strassen's 5C in theory, 2–4 in practice; the 6ND convention takes that constant to be exactly 2.
  • the standard estimate of a run's cost. It ignores attention-score FLOPs (which scale with sequence length, not N), optimizer overhead, and any recomputation — good to a few tens of percent, which is enough to plan with. It is what Lesson 9 waved at when it called the full-dataset gradient "too expensive".
The form with a floor

Hoffmann et al. 2022 (DeepMind, Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — the Chinchilla paper) refitted the whole surface over 400+ runs with one crucial change of shape: an additive constant, so the loss is allowed to stop falling.

  • the irreducible loss — 1.69 nats per token, which is 1.69/ln 2 ≈ 2.44 bits per token. This is Lesson 15's H(p) wearing a fitted hat: the entropy of the text itself, the part of the cross-entropy that no q can remove. Honest caveat: E is estimated by curve-fitting, not measured, and it absorbs anything else that no amount of N or D fixes — tokenizer, architecture ceiling, label noise.
  • the model-capacity term: what you lose for not having enough parameters to represent the conditional distribution. α ≈ 0.34 is about four and a half times steeper than Kaplan's 0.076 — not a contradiction, because a fit with a floor puts all the bending into E and lets the power law be steep.
  • the data term: what you lose for having seen finitely many tokens. It is an estimation-error term in disguise — Lesson 2's variance-of-an-estimator, at the scale of a trillion tokens. β ≈ 0.28.
  • the reducible loss: the sum of the two shrinking terms. The only quantity training can attack. Lesson 15 split the loss the same way — H(p) plus a KL penalty — and Lesson 2 split estimator error the same way again: an irreducible piece plus pieces you can buy down.

Solving the allocation — one Lagrangian

The panel found its minimum by brute force along a curve. Do it properly. Minimize the loss subject to the budget, with a multiplier λ on the constraint:

  • the shadow price of compute: nats of loss saved per extra FLOP. By the envelope theorem λ = −dL★/dC, and the panel prints it — at Chinchilla's budget the marginal FLOP is worth about 6×10−26 nats. Multiply both first-order conditions by N and D respectively and the common value λC drops out; that single step is the whole derivation.
  • the optimality relation: at the optimum the two reducible terms are locked to each other. Rearranged, A N−α : B D−β = β : α = 0.28 : 0.34 — the model term is 0.824× the data term, at every budget. Read it as equal marginal returns: the last FLOP spent on parameters must buy exactly as much loss as the last FLOP spent on tokens.

Substituting D = C/6N into that relation and solving for N gives the result the whole industry re-planned around:

  • the model exponent: 0.28/0.62 = 0.452. With the paper's unrounded α = 0.3392, β = 0.2849 it is 0.4565, which Hoffmann et al. print rounded as a = 0.46.
  • the data exponent: 0.34/0.62 = 0.548 (the paper's b = 0.54). The two exponents sum to 1 by construction, and they are nearly equal: parameters and tokens must grow at almost the same rate. That is the sentence that killed N ∝ C0.73.
  • the frontier exponent for the reducible loss: −0.1535. Spend compute optimally and the gap to the floor falls as C−0.1535. Inverted, halving that gap costs 21/0.1535 ≈ 91× the compute — the economics that funds datacentres.
  • tokens per parameter at the optimum, ∝ C(α−β)/(α+β) = C0.0968. Not a constant: because α ≠ β it drifts upward with budget. Under these printed constants it is ≈26 at 1018 FLOPs and ≈153 at 1026 — a 5.9× drift across eight decades. Hold that thought for the section after next.

The precise statement

Let E, A, B > 0 and α, β > 0, and let L(N,D) = E + A N−α + B D−β. Fix C > 0 and minimize L over the iso-compute curve {6ND = C, N, D > 0}. Substituting D = C/(6N) and writing n = ln N, the objective becomes g(n) = E + A e−αn + B(C/6)−β eβn, a sum of two strictly convex exponentials; hence g is strictly convex, its unique stationary point is the global minimum, and the minimizer is interior. Setting g′(n) = 0 gives the optimality relation αA N−α = βB D−β, equivalently A N−α : B D−β = β : α, independent of C. Solving with the constraint, N★ = (αA / (6ββB))1/(α+β) Cβ/(α+β) and D★ = C/(6N★), so N★ ∝ Cβ/(α+β) and D★ ∝ Cα/(α+β), exponents summing to 1. Consequently L★(C) − E = λ·C·(α+β)/(αβ) ∝ C−αβ/(α+β) where λ = αA N★−α/C = −dL★/dC is the multiplier, and D★/N★ ∝ C(α−β)/(α+β): the token-to-parameter ratio is constant in C if and only if α = β.

Kaplan vs Chinchilla — what actually changed

Two papers, the same architecture family, opposite advice: 0.73/0.27 versus 0.46/0.54. The gap was never a disagreement about scaling — it was bookkeeping, and the first clue is arithmetic you can do yourself. Kaplan's own two exponents do not imply 0.73. Their joint form is L(N,D) = [(Nc/N)p + Dc/D]αD with p = αN/αD = 0.076/0.095 = 0.8; minimize it on 6ND = C exactly as we did above and you get N★ ∝ C1/(1+p) = C5/9 ≈ C0.56 — near-equal exponents, essentially the Chinchilla answer, from the Kaplan surface. The 0.73 lives only in their separate steps-based analysis, and two choices there bend it. First, the losses were read off cosine learning-rate schedules whose length was not matched to the run, so a loss taken mid-schedule — rate still high, model undertrained — is overstated, and which runs get overstated depends on where you sample the curve rather than on any property of scaling; Hoffmann et al. re-tuned the schedule length to each token budget and refitted from finished runs. Second, Kaplan's N counted non-embedding parameters only. At their scale the embedding table is a large share of a small model, so Nnon-emb grows faster than Ntotal along the frontier, and an exponent measured in non-embedding parameters sits above the same exponent measured in total parameters — which is what Chinchilla, and everyone since, reports. Neither is a fact about scaling; both move the slope.

The arithmetic of the correction, in the two models that made the point. Gopher: 280B parameters on 300B tokens — 1.07 tokens per parameter, 6ND ≈ 5.0×1023 FLOPs. Chinchilla: 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens — exactly 20 tokens per parameter, 6ND ≈ 5.9×1023. Same budget to within the slop of the 6ND rule; 4× smaller, 4.67× more tokens, and it beat Gopher across a broad evaluation suite. Read that as the price of a bad allocation: an entire model generation of compute, spent at the wrong point on the curve you were dragging a slider along above.

One honesty note, because the panel computes it and the panel does not lie. Feed the paper's own printed constants into the closed form and the optimum is not 20 tokens per parameter — it is ≈93 at Chinchilla's own budget, drifting as C0.0968. The famous "20" comes from the paper's other two estimation routes (fixed-FLOP minima and training-curve envelopes), which the printed parametric fit does not reproduce; a 2024 replication by Besiroglu and colleagues at Epoch AI documented exactly this inconsistency and refitted the surface with exponents very close to 0.5 each — restoring ≈20 tokens per parameter. What survives all three routes, and is the load-bearing claim, is that the two exponents are near-equal. And the reason the disagreement is survivable is visible in panel one: the valley is flat. At Chinchilla's budget the 20:1 point sits ≈0.007 nats above the printed fit's optimum — under 3% of the reducible loss — for a 4.6× difference in ratio.

Re-applied — the same Lagrangian on a research budget

Strip the language model out and the structure is a budget allocation between two channels of error, each with diminishing returns. A risk model has exactly two such channels: more factors (parameters — finer regime buckets, more drivers per cell) and longer history (data — more days, or more names pooled). A fixed quant-hour and data budget plays the role of C, and the first-order condition is the same sentence:

  • equal marginsthe general form of the Chinchilla condition — no power laws required. Whatever the loss surface, at an interior optimum the marginal error reduction per unit of budget must be equal across channels; otherwise move a unit and improve. αA N−α = βB D−β is just this equality when both channels are power laws.
  • the floorwhatever E is for your problem. For returns it is the part of tomorrow that no function of today's data can touch — Lesson 4's optimal residual, Lesson 1's σ = 100 bp of daily noise standing behind a 5 bp edge. No factor count and no window length removes it, and a model that appears to beat it is telling you about leakage, not skill.

Two honest differences, though, and they both cut against the ML analogy. First, tokens are i.i.d.-ish and interchangeable; history is not. Extending D drags in regimes that no longer apply, so the data term does not fall monotonically — beyond some window the marginal old day has negative value, which the language-model form (B/Dβ, forever decreasing) does not model. Second, nobody has measured α and β for a returns panel, so the exponents here are structure, not numbers. What transfers is the discipline: name the floor, name the two channels, equalize their marginal returns, and stop calling "add more factors" a strategy.

The twist — the most reliable law in ML has no theory, and it is not a law about capability

Scaling laws are the field's most dependable empirical regularity and nobody knows why they hold. There is no accepted first-principles derivation that predicts α ≈ 0.34 from anything; the candidate stories — that the exponent is set by the intrinsic dimension of the data manifold (Lesson 45's manifold hypothesis — Sharma & Kaplan 2020 derive α ≈ 4/d from the intrinsic dimension d of that manifold, by a nearest-neighbour approximation argument), or by the sequential learning of features of decreasing frequency — are suggestive and unproven. Hundreds of billions of dollars of capital expenditure are being allocated against a curve fit.

And they are loss laws, not capability laws. The smoothness lives in the cross-entropy, which averages over every token in the distribution; the things people care about — solve the problem, get the arithmetic exactly right — are thresholded metrics, and a threshold applied to a smoothly improving distribution can jump. That is the mechanism behind the "emergent abilities" of Lesson 24: nothing discontinuous need happen in the loss for a benchmark to go from 0% to 60%. Smooth loss and sharp capability are perfectly compatible, which means a scaling law tells you what your next run's loss will be and almost nothing about what it will be able to do.

Second barrel: the laws break exactly where their assumptions do. The D term assumes fresh tokens. Repeat your corpus and the term degrades — Muennighoff et al. (2023) found a handful of epochs are nearly as good as new data, after which extra repeats add almost nothing and eventually hurt, which is why "we are running out of internet" is a real constraint and not a slogan. Data quality moves A, B and E themselves, so a better corpus is a different curve rather than a point on yours. "Just scale" was never unconditional; it was a statement about one regime, fitted on one kind of data.

Why this matters · volarixs

The Cross-Matrix (Lesson 7) spends a data budget the same way a training run does, and the spending is easy to miss because it looks like modelling rather than allocation. Every extra regime you condition on multiplies the number of cells — parameters — while dividing the sample available to each one: N up, D per cell down, along a curve whose total is fixed by how much honest as-of history exists. That is this lesson's iso-compute curve with the axes relabelled, and the same first-order condition applies: refine the grid only until the marginal cell buys as much accuracy as the marginal observation it costs the others.

You already own the diagnostic for overshooting it. sign_stability — the share of subperiods whose β̂ keeps its sign — is what a starved cell looks like from the outside, and bayesian_pooled_elasticity is the correction that trades a little bias for the variance you bought by splitting too finely (Lesson 2's trade, Lesson 7's panel). The honest boundary: no one has fitted E + A/Nα + B/Dβ to a returns panel, and I would not trust the exponents if they did — non-stationarity breaks the fresh-data assumption harder here than repeated epochs break it for text. What genuinely transfers is the floor and the margin rule: a market's own unpredictability is your E, and past it, extra resolution is pure variance.
