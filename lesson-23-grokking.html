Lesson 23 · Wing V — training-dynamics mysteries

Grokking

The training loss finished two thousand steps ago. Keep going anyway — something is still moving.

Level US GraduateFR Master 2 – PhD

Give a small network a task that has a rule — add two numbers modulo 13 — and show it only some of the pairs. Within a hundred optimizer steps it fits every training pair perfectly; three hundred steps later it is wrong on all 68 held-out pairs. It has built a lookup table. Keep training. The training loss keeps falling — 1.3 × 10−3 by step 800, 2.3 × 10−7 by step 2,000 — long past the point where there is anything left to fit. And two thousand steps after the training set was first mastered, the held-out accuracy that spent its first five hundred steps at or below the 7.7% of pure guessing crosses 90% — 63 of the 68 — and by step 2,750 it has every pair right. The network groks: it swaps the memorised table for an algorithm. Two things make this worth a lesson. The mechanism is now understood — it is weight decay, still pulling on parameters the fit term has stopped constraining. And the algorithm has been read out of the weights: the network puts its inputs on a circle and adds angles, the trick Lesson 18's RoPE hard-codes by hand. Everything below trains live in your browser from a fixed seed; nothing here is a drawn curve.

The words, before the machinery

Nine plain-English terms. Nothing is a theorem yet.

  • modular additionthe task: given a and b in {0, 1, …, P−1}, output (a + b) mod P — the remainder after dividing by P. Here P = 13, so there are 13 × 13 = 169 possible pairs and 13 possible answers. Guessing scores 1/13 = 7.7%.
  • memorisefit the training pairs by storing them, one at a time, with nothing carried over to unseen pairs. A perfectly legal solution: with enough parameters a network can hold an arbitrary table. It scores 100% on what it saw and chance on what it did not.
  • generalisefit the training pairs by computing something that happens to be right everywhere. Also 100% on the training set — the two solutions are indistinguishable from the training loss alone. That indistinguishability is the whole lesson.
  • weight decaya term λ‖θ‖²/2 added to the loss, where θ is every parameter stacked into one long vector and ‖θ‖ its Euclidean length. λ ≥ 0 sets the price of size — λ/2 per unit of squared parameter. It is ridge regression's penalty (Lesson 5) applied to a network, and its gradient is simply λθ — a constant pull toward the origin.
  • zero-loss manifoldthe set of parameter vectors that fit the training data exactly. Not a point: an overparameterised model has a whole surface of exact fits, and both the memoriser and the generaliser live on it. Moving along it changes nothing about the training loss and everything about the test loss.
  • grok stepthe first step at which held-out accuracy exceeds 90%. An arbitrary but fixed threshold — the panels use it as a stopwatch. The interesting number is its gap from the step where training accuracy first hit 100%. The phenomenon was first reported by Power et al. (2022) on exactly this family of small algorithmic datasets.
  • natsnatural-log units of surprise, the units of the cross-entropy loss of Lesson 15. Divide by ln 2 ≈ 0.693 for bits. A model that guesses uniformly among 13 answers pays ln 13 = 2.565 nats per example.
  • frequency, DFT powerwrite a list of 13 numbers indexed by a as a sum of waves cos(2πka/13) and sin(2πka/13) for k = 1 … 6; the power at k is how much of the list that wave pair explains. The discrete Fourier transform, on 13 points. Six frequencies is all there is: k and 13 − k are the same wave.
  • progress measurea number computed from the weights that moves smoothly while accuracy is flat. Nanda et al. (2023) built their account of exactly this phenomenon around one; the panels below use the same idea, and it is what turns "sudden jump" into "visible ramp".
The task, the model, and the two ways to solve it

Inputs are one-hot: the pair (a, b) picks row a of one embedding table and row b of a second. The two rows are added, the result is squared coordinate-wise, and a linear read-out produces 13 logits. Squaring is the cheapest activation that can multiply two things together, and multiplying is what an angle-addition identity needs; it is also the setting in which Gromov (2023) writes the generalising solution in closed form.

  • two embedding tables, each 13 × 48: row a is the vector the network uses to stand for the input a. These are the objects the second panel dissects. Lesson 20's slogan — a feature is a direction — is the reason looking at rows is the right move.
  • hidden unit j, H = 48 of them: the square of the summed embeddings. Expanding (u+v)² = u² + 2uv + v² manufactures the cross term uv from an addition — the only nonlinearity in the model, and the one that lets a and b interact at all.
  • the read-out, 13 × 48: logit for answer c is a weighted sum of hidden units. Because h is quadratic in the embeddings, zc is a quadratic form; neurons taken in ± pairs cancel the u² and v² halves and leave the cross term alone, so with H = 48 ≥ 2P the logits for any one answer can be an arbitrary 13 × 13 table — which is exactly why memorising is available to this model.
  • the predicted probability of answer c — softmax of the logits, Lesson 17's normaliser used as a classifier head. 1,872 parameters in total: 2 × 13 × 48 embedding entries plus 13 × 48 read-out entries.

The fitting loss is Lesson 15's cross-entropy in nats, averaged over the training pairs, and the objective actually descended adds the decay term:

  • the training subset of the 169 pairs, a fixed seeded random sample of size ρ·169. ρ is the second slider below. The remaining pairs are the held-out set — never touched by a gradient.
  • the decay strength, the first slider. λ = 0 removes the second term entirely and the objective becomes pure fit. Everything this lesson is about is downstream of that one switch.
  • the squared Euclidean norm of all 1,872 parameters. A single scalar summarising "how big are the weights". At the seeded initialisation used here it is 65.19² — the panels print ‖θ‖ itself, not its square.

Run it to the end and the default does not sit at a clean sweep: it takes all 68 at step 2,750, gives two back, and holds 66 of 68 — 97.1% — from step 3,500 to the end of the budget. Grokked means the algorithm is in place, not that the last pair is nailed down forever. Three settings are worth doing by hand. λ = 0: the run memorises at step 100 and stays there — at step 6,000 it is right on 2 of the 68 held-out pairs, worse than the 5.2 that guessing would give, and its test loss is 109.5 nats against a training loss of 10−11. That is not a model on its way somewhere; that is a finished lookup table. ρ = 0.40 (68 training pairs): still a memoriser at 6,000 steps, 12.9% test. ρ = 0.55 (93 pairs) reaches 84.2% but never crosses the 90% line inside the budget, while ρ = 0.60 (101 pairs) crosses it at step 2,100 — the data-quantity boundary is between those two, and it is sharp.

The mechanism — norm descent on the zero-loss manifold

Once the training loss is numerically zero its gradient is numerically zero too, and the descent direction is whatever is left:

  • the fit term has stopped talking. In the default run the training loss is 5.7 × 10−2 at step 400, 1.3 × 10−3 at step 800 and 2.3 × 10−7 at step 2,000 — five orders of magnitude gone while held-out accuracy is still climbing.
  • the only surviving force: shrink, uniformly, toward the origin. Exponential decay of the norm is what this says in isolation. In practice the fit term reasserts itself the moment shrinking starts to break the fit, so the true motion is a slide along the zero-loss set — decay tangentially, fit normally. The dotted line above shows the difference: ‖θ‖ drops 65.19 → 17.8 by step 250 while decay dominates, then climbs back to 26.1 by step 1,800 as the fit term re-tightens. The exponential is the isolated force, not the trajectory.
  • AdamWthe optimizer these panels use. Loshchilov & Hutter's decoupled form applies the decay as θ ← (1 − ηλ)θ outside the adaptive rescaling, so when the fit gradient vanishes the update is exactly a multiplicative shrink. Plain gradient descent on 𝓛 gives the same force; Adam's normalised steps (Lesson 9) are what keep the run moving at all when the raw gradient is 10−7.

So the question stops being "which minimum does the optimizer find" and becomes "which point on the zero-loss set has the smallest norm". The measured answer, from the two runs above: the memoriser sits at ‖θ‖ = 62.8 and the generaliser at ‖θ‖ = 25.2 — both with training loss below 10−6 (9.7 × 10−12 and 1.8 × 10−7 respectively), a factor 2.5 apart in size. The generalising circuit produces the same training logits out of a smaller parameter vector, which is the argument Varma et al. (2023) make quantitatively under the name circuit efficiency. Weight decay is not repairing overfitting here; it is a preference over solutions that only becomes decisive once fitting is finished.

The precise statement

Three claims of three different kinds. (i) Elementary. For the regularised objective 𝓛(θ) = 𝓛fit(θ) + (λ/2)‖θ‖², gradient flow obeys d‖θ‖²/dt = −2⟨θ, ∇𝓛fit⟩ − 2λ‖θ‖². On the interpolating set M = {θ : 𝓛fit(θ) = 0}, which for a smooth overparameterised model is generically a manifold of positive dimension rather than a point, ∇𝓛fit vanishes, so at a point of M the only force is −λθ; whatever part of it points off M is opposed by the fit term the instant it acts, and the surviving displacement is tangential — a slide along M, not out of a basin. Grokking is therefore not an escape from a local minimum — nothing is escaped, because nothing was trapped; it is displacement along a set on which the training objective is constant. (ii) Empirical, and the point of the panels. Memorising and generalising parameter vectors both lie in M, so the training loss cannot separate them; they are separated by ‖θ‖, and in the run above by a factor of 2.5 (62.8 against 25.2, both at 𝓛fit < 10−6). With λ = 0 the run initialised at ‖θ‖ = 65.2 reaches M at a memorising point and stays there indefinitely. That the generalising solution has the smaller norm is a measured fact about this task and this architecture, not a theorem. (iii) Empirical reverse-engineering (Nanda et al., 2023). For modular addition, trained networks implement a discrete Fourier transform: input a is embedded as (cos 2πk a/P, sin 2πk a/P) for a small set of key frequencies k, hidden units combine the two embeddings through a product-to-sum identity so that a term in 2πk(a+b)/P appears, and the read-out scores answer c by Σk cos(2πk(a+b−c)/P), which is maximised exactly at c ≡ a + b (mod P). Morwani et al. (2024) prove that for one-hidden-layer networks on this task the maximum-margin solutions are of this Fourier form, which is the closest thing to a theorem in the neighbourhood; the claim that trained networks converge to it remains an experimental finding.

The algorithm it finds — angle addition

Why should a circle be the efficient answer? Because modular addition is rotation. Put input a at angle 2πka/13 on a circle; then adding a and b modulo 13 is composing two rotations, and rotations compose by adding angles. The model has one nonlinearity, squaring, and squaring a sum of two cosines produces exactly the required cross term:

  • the phase neuron j assigns to input a: a wave of frequency k with the neuron's own offset ψj. This is what "row a of the embedding table" becomes once the table is sinusoidal — the same object Lesson 18 builds by hand, except here nobody built it.
  • the only useful term: it depends on a and b solely through a + b. Product-to-sum, 2cos α cos β = cos(α−β) + cos(α+β) — the identity a trigonometry course teaches and a network rediscovers. The other three terms are noise the read-out must cancel, which it does by summing neurons whose offsets ψj are spread around the circle.
  • the score for answer c, summed over key frequencies. Every term is 1 when c ≡ a + b and the terms interfere destructively otherwise — a Dirichlet kernel peaked at the right answer. Take the argmax and you have the algorithm.

Lesson 18 is the same identity used the other way round. RoPE hard-codes rotations so that attention scores depend on the gap n − m; here the network discovers the encoding because it is the cheapest way to compute a sum. Two different pressures, one geometry.

One honest deflation. Nanda et al. found about five key frequencies out of the 56 available at P = 113 — genuine sparsity, most of the Fourier basis unused. At P = 13 there are only six frequencies in existence and this run uses all six, roughly evenly. What survives at this scale is not the sparsity of the frequency set but the purity of each neuron: at the end of the default run the mean single-frequency share φ is 0.862, against 0.408 for random weights — and 0.408 is not a fudge, it is the exact expectation H6/6 = (1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + ⅕ + ⅙)/6 of max-over-sum for six i.i.d. exponential powers, which is what the DFT of a Gaussian column gives. The λ = 0 memoriser finishes at φ = 0.423. Statistically, its embedding is noise.

The twist — the discontinuity is in the metric, not in the model

Grokking looks like a switch flipping. It is not. Run the default configuration and watch φ, the Fourier purity, rather than the accuracy: it is 0.435 at initialisation, 0.548 at step 400, and 0.648 at step 600 — exactly half of the journey from that starting value to its final 0.862 is already complete. At step 600 the held-out accuracy is 7.4%, which is 5 pairs out of 68, below the 7.7% you get by guessing. The structure was being built the whole time; accuracy could not see it, because accuracy is an argmax — a thresholded functional of the logits, blind to the difference between a wrong answer and a nearly-right one. This is precisely the illusion behind the emergent abilities of Lesson 24 and the loss-versus-capability split at the end of Lesson 21: a smooth internal quantity read through a threshold produces a step. Progress measures exist to undo that, and the panel above is one.

Second barrel: grokking is not exotic. Lesson 22's double descent is the story of test error getting worse and then better as parameters increase past the interpolation point, with regularisation deciding which of the many interpolating solutions you land on. Grokking is that same story told along the time axis instead of the parameter axis. In both, the model reaches zero training error early; in both, the interesting motion afterwards is a slide among solutions that are indistinguishable to the training loss; in both, regularisation is the tiebreaker and it wins slowly. The units on the horizontal axis differ. The mechanism does not.

Re-applied — the backtest that stops too early

Strip the modular arithmetic away and the structure is a familiar one. A strategy can fit an in-sample window perfectly two ways: by memorising idiosyncratic events — this drawdown, that squeeze, the specific week the correlation inverted — or by capturing a mechanism that happens to have produced those events. Both reach the same in-sample objective, so the in-sample number cannot tell you which you have, exactly as the training loss above cannot separate the memoriser from the generaliser. What separates them is size: the memorising fit needs large, finely-tuned, mutually-cancelling coefficients, and the mechanism fit does not. That is why the honest search is over 𝓛fit + (λ/2)‖θ‖² and not over 𝓛fit, and it is the same ridge penalty of Lesson 5 doing the same job.

The transferable observation is narrower than the analogy, so state it narrowly. The signature that the panels above make visible — out-of-sample performance improving long after in-sample performance stopped improving — is a signature a conventional backtest is structurally incapable of showing you, because a conventional backtest stops when in-sample convergence stops. Whether anything analogous to grokking actually occurs in a returns model is a different and much weaker claim, and I would not make it: grokking as a phenomenon needs an exact rule to be discovered, and returns do not have one. What does carry over is the design instruction — do not use in-sample convergence as the stopping rule, and do not read a stable in-sample fit as evidence that the search has finished choosing.

Why this matters · volarixs

The Cross-Matrix (Lesson 7) fits one elasticity per regime cell, and a thinly-populated cell is precisely a small dataset on which an unpenalised fit will memorise. The mechanism above says what to do about it and why: two coefficient vectors can hit the same in-sample residual, and the one with the smaller norm is the one that will still be there next quarter. bayesian_pooled_elasticity is that norm preference implemented as shrinkage toward the pooled estimate — the λ term of this lesson, with a prior instead of the origin as the target.

sign_stability — the share of subperiods in which β̂ keeps its sign — is then the diagnostic that plays the role held-out accuracy plays here: it is the number that separates the two solutions after the in-sample fit has stopped distinguishing them. The boundary worth respecting: nothing in this lesson licenses training a cell longer and expecting a late transition. The transferable content is the ordering — fit first, then choose among fits by norm — not the timing.
